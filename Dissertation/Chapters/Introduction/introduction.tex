\chapter*{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
The interdisciplinary nature of science has enabled nuclear physics to play an increasingly tangible and impactful role in providing greater benefits to society. This thesis work is framed within this context, delving into the nuclear fragmentation processes induced by the interaction of nuclei with matter. Nuclear fragmentation is indeed of interest not only for fundamental nuclear physics, but also for two central medical and technological applications, namely Particle Therapy (PT) and Space Radiation Protection (SRP).

PT (or hadrontherapy) is an advanced form of radiotherapy used in oncological treatment which involves the irradiation of cancerous regions with hadronic particle beams to damage the DNA of tumor cells. As hadronic particles penetrate human tissues, they deposit most of their energy precisely at the tumor site (the Bragg peak), sparing contiguous healthy tissues. As the dose conformity of particle therapy is superior to conventional X-ray treatments, PT is particularly notable in the treatment of radio-resistant and deep-seated tumors, as the latter are extremely difficult to access with surgical intervention. Hence, particle therapy represents a high-growth sector involving numerous research areas, such as theragnostic, FLASH therapy and Minibeam proton therapy. A critical challenge in PT is the nuclear fragmentation induced by beam-tissue interactions, as the biological effects of the resulting secondary fragments have yet to be fully assessed. Measuring the missing fragmentation cross sections will benchmark and update the existing nuclear models implemented in Monte Carlo transport codes, reducing dose delivery uncertainties and improving the assessment of radiation-induced hazards during treatment planning.

Alongside PT, nuclear fragmentation is hugely relevant to the field of SRP, which focuses on developing protective materials that shield against ionizing radiation coming from cosmic sources. Contrary to PT, nuclear fragments produced by cosmic radiation (mainly galactic cosmic rays and Solar Particle Events) pose a threat not only to humans but also to the electronic equipment deployed in long-term space missions. Consequently, national space agencies are increasingly supporting nuclear fragmentation measurements, driven by their growing interest in space missions beyond the Low Earth Orbit, which include manned missions to Mars.

In the context of nuclear fragmentation measurements, few beam-target-energy combinations have been explored, leading to a lack of experimental data. To compensate for this data deficit, the FOOT (FragmentatiOn Of Target) experiment aims to precisely measure nuclear fragment double differential cross sections in both fragment production angle and kinetic energy, addressing the critical scarcity of reliable fragmentation cross section data. Specifically, the FOOT experiment focuses on ions with nuclear charge number $Z\leq8$ and kinetic energies $100$--$800\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$, and measures fragmentation cross sections of nuclear interactions between ion beams (mainly Carbon and Oxygen) and fixed targets of interest for both PT (Hydrogen, Carbon and Oxygen) and SRP (hydrogen-enriched) with a maximum uncertainty of $5\%$ for projectile fragmentation and $10\%$ for target fragmentation.

Achieving such precision demands a continuous optimization of each FOOT subdetector. This thesis work, developed within the FOOT Bologna group, addresses two complementary aspects of the FOOT tracking system, at both the hardware and software levels. On the hardware side, the thesis develops and validates the \texttt{VHDL} firmware for the DE10-Nano trigger board (TrgBox) to manage signal synchronization, trigger-busy distribution and readout control for the MIMOSIS-2.1 sensors of the forthcoming Vertex Tracker (VT), which will be integrated in the FOOT electronic setup as of November 2026. On the software side, developments on the FOOT global tracking system are presented analyzing the CNAO 2025 data-taking campaign, which studies nuclear fragmentation by irradiating $200\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ carbon ions on a $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ carbon target. The developed work, driven by the study of pull distributions (i.e., normalized residuals), is pertinent to the position reconstruction and the alignment of the current MIMOSA-28 VT and the Microstrip Silicon Detector (MSD), which are optimized within the SHOE framework, the reconstruction software maintained by the FOOT Collaboration. The effect of these changes on the global track fit is assessed through $\chi^2$ and $p$-value distributions. Finally, the momentum resolution is measured on experimental data.

The thesis structure is organized as follows. \autoref{ch:interaction_charged_particles} reviews the interactions of charged particles with matter, which involve both electromagnetic and nuclear interactions. Particular attention is devoted to the nuclear fragmentation processes and on their implications for PT and SRP. The most relevant radiobiological aspects are also outlined in \autoref{ch:radiobiology}. \autoref{ch:foot_experiment} presents a more thorough description of the scientific motivation, the operation and the experimental setup of the FOOT experiment. To this regard, \autoref{ch:chi_squared_min} and \autoref{ch:alm_squared_min} analyze respectively the methods employed by FOOT to improve the mass identification of nuclear fragments. \autoref{ch:trigger_board} described the new MIMOSIS-2.1 Vertex Tracker and the implementation of the TrgBox firmware. Finally, \autoref{ch:analysis_vt_msd_momentum} details the SHOE tracking software and the optimization of the VT and MSD, concluding with the performance studies of global tracking and the momentum resolution results.
