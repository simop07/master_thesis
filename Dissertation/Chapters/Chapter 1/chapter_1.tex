\chapter{Interactions of charged particles with matter}\label{ch:interaction_charged_particles}
The clinical methods and the research studies developed in Particle Therapy (PT, or hadrontherapy) and Space Radiation Protection (SRP) strongly depend on deterministic codes and Monte Carlo simulations, whose aim is to emulate the physics processes characterizing such applications. To comprehensively model the phenomena underlying PT and SRP, including the biological effects produced by ionizing radiation, scientists have implemented algorithms based on the physics principles of particle interaction. Thus, the aim of this chapter is to outline the relevant interaction mechanisms between charged particles and matter, emphasizing the physics and biologic processes that dominate the PT and SRP energy ranges.

Before delving into the details of charged particles interactions with matter, it is important to recall that at the energy ranges of PT ($100$--$800\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$) and of SRP ($100$--$400\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$) charged particles are moderately relativistic. Indeed, for a charged particle with kinetic energy $E_\text{kin}$, total energy $E_\text{tot}$, mass $m$ and momentum $p$, the particle velocity $\beta$ in units of light speed $c$ is given by \autoref{eq:particle_velocity}:
\begin{equation}
	\beta
	=
	\frac{v}{c}
	=
	\frac{pc}{E_{\text{tot}}}
	=
	\frac{\sqrt{E_{\text{tot}}^2-m^2c^4}}
	{E_{\text{kin}}+mc^2}
	=
	\frac{\sqrt{E_{\text{kin}}^2+2E_{\text{kin}}mc^2}}
	{E_{\text{kin}}+mc^2}.
	\label{eq:particle_velocity}
\end{equation}
As particles with the same energy per nucleon have the same $\beta$ (this stems directly from \autoref{eq:particle_velocity}), it is possible to compute $\beta$ values simply using the proton mass ($938\,\mathrm{MeV/c}^2$) and kinetic energy, obtaining $\beta\simeq0.6$ at $200\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ and $\beta\simeq0.8$ at $800\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$.

A charged particle can interact with matter following the electromagnetic and nuclear processes listed below, which will be detailed in the following paragraphs:
\begin{itemize}
	\item electromagnetic interactions, further divided in:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item inelastic collisions with atomic electrons (see \autoref{ssec:bethe_bloch});
		\item elastic scattering with nuclei (multiple Coulomb scattering, see \autoref{ssec:multiple_coulomb_scattering});
		\item Cherenkov radiation emission and bremsstrahlung (negligible in both PT and SRP applications);
	\end{itemize}
	\item inelastic nuclear interactions (see \autoref{sec:nucl_interactions}).
\end{itemize}
The inelastic collisions with atomic electrons represent the dominant interaction mechanism undergone by charged particles, as the high cross section values ($\simeq100\,\mathrm{Mb}$) show.\footnote{The barn (b) is the unit of measurement of cross section. Specifically, $1\,\mathrm{b}=10^{-24}\,\mathrm{cm}^{2}$.} Due to the large cross sections, a large number of collision occurs between the incoming charged particles and the atomic electrons, making any cross-section-based analytical treatment impractical. For this reason, an energy-based study is preferred: rather than studying whether a particle has interacted or not, scientists analyze the energy released by the particle inside the medium. These continuous electromagnetic energy losses slow down the incoming particle, which comes to rest once its kinetic energy zeroes. It should be noted that the inelastic collisions with atomic electrons make the interaction dynamics of charged particles and photons profoundly different: photons can only be absorbed when interacting with matter\footnote{To be precise, photon have also a non-zero probability to loose energy during a scattering process without necessarily being absorbed, as in Compton scattering. However, photon energy losses occur through one-shot, discrete events instead of continuous interactions.} rather than unceasingly loosing energy, as charged particles do. The very particle-photon diversity produces longitudinal energy deposition profiles, whose dissimilarities truly embody the contrast between PT and conventional photon-based radiotherapy, as detailed in \autoref{ssec:range}.

Contrary to the inelastic collisions with atomic electrons, the nuclear elastic scattering (which involves nuclei despite being an electromagnetic process) does not lead to significant energy losses, as the nuclei involved in elastic collisions usually have comparable masses to those of the incident particles. This implies that the elastic nuclear interactions transfer less energy compared to the collisions with atomic electrons. Nevertheless, the elastic scattering with nuclei produces electromagnetic deflections, which in PT broaden the beam lateral profile. The typical cross section of this process is approximately $\simeq1\,\mathrm{b}$, lower than the former case.

Inelastic nuclear reactions (which include nuclear fragmentations) affect both the longitudinal and transverse profiles of the energy deposition, due to the fact that these processes lead to beam reductions and subsequent creation of secondary particles. Nuclear interactions are less probable than the aforementioned electromagnetic collisions, as the former are characterized by cross sections of $\simeq10$--$100\,\mathrm{mb}$. Such large discrepancy in cross section values stems from the fact that the atomic radius is $10^4$ times larger than the nucleus radius.
\section{Electromagnetic interactions}\label{sec:em_interactions}
\subsection{Inelastic collisions with atomic electrons}\label{ssec:bethe_bloch}
When a relativistic charged particle moves through matter, it exerts a Coulomb force on the atomic electrons of the medium, leading to numerous excitation and ionization processes \cite{Christodoulides2016}. Excitation allows orbital electrons to jump to higher atomic level, while ionization strips the electron away from the atom. If ejected electrons have sufficient energy, they may further ionize the medium and, in that case, they are referred to as $\delta$ rays (or $\delta$ electrons). Most frequently the energy losses are small (for $90\%$ of all collisions the energy losses are less than $100\,\mathrm{eV}$) \cite{passageParticleMatter}. As previously mentioned, the amount of energy lost due to Coulomb interactions with nuclei is even smaller \cite{10.1063/1.369844}, hence it is overlooked in the following discussion. For a moderately relativistic charged particle, the mean rate of energy loss (or linear stopping power) leading to electron excitation and ionization processes can be described by the Bethe-Bloch equation \cite{KolanoskiBook}:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\left\langle -\frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle
		={}&
		\frac{\rho Z_\mathrm{M}}{A}
		\frac{4\pi N_A m_e c^2}{M_U}
		\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 m_e c^2}\right)^2
		\frac{Z^2}{\beta^2}\cdot
		\\
		&\quad\cdot
		\left[
		\frac{1}{2}
		\ln\left(
		\frac{2m_e c^2\beta^2\gamma^2 T_\mathrm{max}}{I^2}
		\right)
		-\beta^2
		-\frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2}
		-\frac{C(\beta\gamma,I)}{Z}
		\right],
	\end{split}
	\label{eq:bethe_bloch}
\end{equation}
whose parameters are summarized in \autoref{tab:bethe_bloch}. Note that the negative sign in $\left\langle-dE/dx \right\rangle$ represents the amount of energy lost by the charged particle. Hereafter, the $\left\langle-dE/dx \right\rangle$ expression is also indicated as $dE/dx$ for simplicity of notation.

The \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch} is valid in the region $0.1\lesssim\beta\gamma=p/Mc\lesssim1000$ with an accuracy of few percent, where $\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$ is the Lorentz factor and $M$ is the incoming particle mass. At $\beta\gamma\simeq0.1$ the projectile speed is comparable to the atomic electron speed, and at $\beta\gamma\simeq1000$ radiative effects start to dominate. Both limit are $Z_\text{M}$ dependent, while a minor dependence on the incoming particle mass $M$ is introduced at high energies through $I$. To remove the dependencies on the medium density $\rho$, the mass stopping power is often employed, defined as:
\begin{equation}
	\text{Mass stopping power}\,=\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot\left\langle-\frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle,\quad\text{ in }[\text{MeV}\cdot\text{g}^{-1}\cdot\text{cm}^2].
	\label{eq:mass_stopping_power}
\end{equation}
As illustrated in \autoref{fig:mass_stopping_power}, the mass stopping power is approximately independent of the material and its physical state, except for slowly decreasing with $Z_\text{M}$.\footnote{Despite $dE/dx\propto Z_\text{M}$, it decreases with $Z_\text{M}$ because of the presence of $A$ in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}: heavier materials have decreasing $Z_\text{M}/A$ ratios. In general, a simple functional dependence on $Z_\text{M}$ is not to be expected \cite{passageParticleMatter}.}

	\caption[Bethe-Bloch curve in different materials]{Mass stopping power in various materials. Radiative effects (relevant for muons and pions) are not included. From \cite{passageParticleMatter}.}
	\label{fig:mass_stopping_power}
\end{figure}
The functional dependence of the mass stopping power and the Bethe-Bloch formula on $\beta\gamma$ is illustrated in \autoref{fig:bethe_bloch}. To analyze their behavior, it is possible to define various energy regions \cite{Christodoulides2016}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item at very low energies, the kinetic energy of the incident particle must be corrected by the factor $C$ (shell correction), already reported in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}, which accounts for energy effects due to the motion of the electron around the nucleus. Such correction is needed because the Bethe-Bloch formula is derived assuming that the incoming particles have (relativistic) speeds much larger than the atomic electron velocities (the Born approximation), a premise which holds no more at low energies. The shell correction lowers the $Z$ of the incoming particles, as they get partly neutralized by picking up electrons when traveling at low speeds. In this low-energy region, there are also other minor corrections such as the Barkas effect, which accounts for Coulomb corrections caused by the charge of the projectile particle, and the Bloch correction, which introduces second-order perturbations to atomic wave functions. At $\beta\lesssim0.5$, Lindhard corrections and Andersen-Ziegler formulae adequately describe the mass stopping power.
	\item Classical region: at low energies ($\beta\gamma<1$) the stopping power decreases as $\frac{1}{\beta^2}$, since faster charged particles spend less time near the atoms of the material and therefore generate fewer ionization processes.
	\item Minimum ionization region: at higher energies ($\beta\gamma\approx3$) a minimum in ionization is observed, depending on $\beta$ and (almost) independent of the mass $M$ of the charged particle. Most relativistic particles have mean energy loss rates close to the minimum, in which case they are usually referred to as Minimum Ionizing Particles (MIPs).
	\item Relativistic region: at high energies ($\beta\gamma>4$) a logarithmic rise in the stopping power is observed, due to the concentration of the electric field lines in the orthogonal plane with respect to the incident particle direction, a purely relativistic effect. The behavior of this region is governed by the explicit logarithmic term $\beta^2\gamma^2$ in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch} and also implicitly by $T_\text{max}$ dependence on $\beta^2\gamma$:
	\begin{equation}
		T_\text{max}=\frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2}{1+2\gamma m_e/M+(m_e/M)^2}.
		\label{eq:t_max}
	\end{equation}
	\item At very high energies, the concentration of electric field lines induces further ionization events at larger distances from the particle's trajectory. As more distant atoms loose electrons, they get polarized, producing a screening effect on the electric field itself (called the density effect) that limits the field extension and effectively truncates the relativistic rise (see the red dotted line in \autoref{fig:bethe_bloch}). The density effect is controlled by the factor $\delta$ (density correction) in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}.
\end{itemize}

	\caption[Bethe-Bloch curve]{Absolute value of the stopping power for $\mu^+$ in copper as a function of $\beta\gamma=p/Mc$, where $p$ is the magnitude of $\mu^+$ relativistic momentum. In particular, the dashed green line labeled ``$\mu^+$'' accounts for the Barkas effect, while the dashed red line represents the action of the density effect. From \cite{passageParticleMatter}.}

\end{figure}
Note that the Bethe-Bloch formula in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch} also includes the energy loss caused by Cherenkov radiation \cite{KolanoskiBook}, being part of the electromagnetic interactions that a charged particle may undergo. However, the Cherenkov contribution is small ($\simeq1\,\mathrm{keV}\cdot\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{/g}$) compared to the energy lost due to ionization $\simeq1$--$2\,\mathrm{MeV}\cdot\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{/g}$. Furthermore, transition radiation is generally not included in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}, as homogeneous media are assumed in its derivation. Finally, \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch} does not include energy loss by bremsstrahlung, which becomes dominant at high $\beta\gamma$ values \cite{KolanoskiBook}.
\subsection{Statistical fluctuations of energy loss}\label{ssec:stat_fluct}
The Bethe-Bloch formula describes the average energy loss per path length. The necessity of defining an ``average'' value stems from the intrinsic stochastic nature of energy loss processes, which exhibit fluctuations. The energy loss $\Delta E$ along a distance $\Delta x$ is composed of many small random contributions $\delta E_n$, each corresponding to a specific ionization process:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta E = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta E_n.
	\label{eq:stochastic_bethe}
\end{equation}
The randomness of the Bethe-Bloch derives from two fluctuations: the first is related to the energy variations in the amount $\delta E_n$ transferred in each ionization process, while the second is connected to the effective total number $N$ of excitation/ionization processes (dependent on Poisson statistics). The Bethe-Bloch formula provides the mean value of these energy losses, however their statistical nature allows for a more detailed description through probability distributions. In general, the fluctuations in the energy loss lead to asymmetric distributions with a Gaussian component, which models the many ionizations with tiny energy transfers, and a tail, located at larger energy loss values. The Gaussian component and the tail are visible in \autoref{fig:asymmetric_dist}. The heavy tail of \autoref{fig:asymmetric_dist} is generated by rare high-ionization events (especially central/hard collisions) where charged particle transfer a large amount of energy, inducing the formation of $\delta$ electrons that further ionize the medium. As shown in \autoref{fig:asymmetric_dist}, the skewness of the distribution allows to distinguish the most probable value of the energy loss (indicated as $\Delta_p$ in the following), corresponding to the maximum of the distribution, and the average energy loss $\left\langle dE/dx \right\rangle$, which is effectively the value estimated by \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}. 
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{asymmetric_dist.pdf}
	\caption[Asymmetric energy loss distribution]{Distribution of measured $dE/dx$ values of MIP pions ($p = 400$--$800\,\mathrm{MeV/c}$). The most probable $dE/dx$ value is located at the distribution maximum (at $6.8\,\mathrm{keV/cm}$). The average $dE/dx$ values is shown as well, along with the value above which $30\%$ of the largest energy loss entries are located. From \cite{KolanoskiBook}.}
	\label{fig:asymmetric_dist}
\end{figure}
The exact form of the energy loss distribution depend on \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}, \autoref{eq:t_max} and mainly on the absorber medium thickness. For example, if the particles were to pass through thin layers of low-density materials, the fluctuations in energy loss per unit length would be well described by a Landau distribution:
\begin{equation}
	f(\Delta E,\rho x)d\Delta E =
	\frac{1}{\langle\Delta E\rangle}\phi_L(\lambda_L)d\lambda_L,
	\label{eq:landau_dist}
\end{equation}
whereas for thick layers of high-density matter the distribution would be substantially Gaussian due to the larger number of collisions (that allow the application of the central limit theorem):
\begin{equation}
	f(\Delta E) =
	\frac{1}{\sigma_{\Delta E}\sqrt{2\pi}}
	\exp\left(
	-\frac{(\Delta E-\overline{\Delta E})^2}
	{2\sigma_{\Delta E}^2}
	\right).
	\label{eq:gaussian_dist}
\end{equation}
In \autoref{eq:landau_dist} and \autoref{eq:gaussian_dist}, $f(\Delta E,\rho x)$ and $f(\Delta E)$ represent the probability density functions of the Landau and Gaussian distributions, respectively. In \autoref{eq:landau_dist}, $\rho$ and $x$ are respectively the mass density and the thickness of the absorber medium, while the $\phi_L(\lambda_L)$ function and the $\lambda_L$ parameter are further contributions calculated by Landau in \cite{NAP20066}. $\langle\Delta E\rangle$ and $\overline{\Delta E}$ are respectively the approximate mean energy loss and the Gaussian mean energy loss, while $\sigma_{\Delta E}$ is the Gaussian distribution standard deviation. A more comprehensive mathematical theory able to describe the distribution of energy losses in detail was given by Vavilov in \cite{Vavilov:1957zz}.

The choice of the energy loss distribution is fundamental to correctly estimate the energy fluctuations produced by a traversing charged particle, particularly in the field of PT, as detailed in \autoref{ssec:range}. More generally, these fluctuations are unfavorable in the field of particle physics \cite{KolanoskiBook}, as:
\begin{itemize}
	\item fluctuations limit the resolution on energy-momentum measurements, leading particles to loose energy during the momentum measurement. For instance, unwanted energy losses lead to increasing track curvatures, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:changed_curv};
	\item fluctuations broaden the overall width of $dE/dx$ distributions, worsening the discrimination capability of charged particles identified through $dE/dx$ measurements;
	\item fluctuations degrade the spatial resolution in tracking detectors, increasing the shape irregularities of ionization clusters. The high-ionization events generating knocked-on $\delta$ electrons further complicate the spatial precision, as $\delta$ rays move away from the track and produce further secondary ionization.
\end{itemize}
To suppress energy deposit fluctuations and define an unbiased energy loss estimator, two approaches can be followed:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item prefer the most probable value $\Delta_p$ of the Landau distribution to the average $\left\langle dE/dx \right\rangle$, as the former is more stable than the latter. This can be explained looking at the three bottom curves shown in \autoref{fig:fermi_plateau}, which display the most probable energy losses $\Delta_p$ per path length $x$ for silicon layers of various thicknesses. From the plot it is clear that the average energy loss of the Bethe-Bloch formula (solid red line) rises with the particle energy, whereas the $\Delta_p/x$ is nearly independent of it. The stability region where $\Delta_p/x$ is approximately constant is called ``Fermi plateau''.
	\item Introduce a restricted energy loss, defined including only the energy losses up to a fixed energy threshold $T_\text{cut}$, as shown in \autoref{fig:asymmetric_dist}. In practice, if one wants to reduce the influence of the Landau tail at larger ionization events, it is possible to adopt the truncated mean method. Truncating the values above a certain $T_\text{cut}$ means calculating the average energy loss only using the lower remaining entries. The measured average energy loss is then ``restricted'' to the smallest values of the Landau distribution. This truncation procedure makes the average energy loss closer to the most probable value: consequently, the restricted energy loss saturates at high energies reaching the Fermi plateau (see \autoref{fig:fermi_plateau}), similarly to the most probable energy loss $\Delta_p$. The restricted energy loss method may be used also to improve the position measurement, limiting the cluster size to suppress $\delta$ electrons emitted perpendicular to a particle track.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Range and longitudinal straggling}\label{ssec:range}
The Bethe-Bloch formula in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch} can be integrated to introduce the Continuous Slowing-Down Approximation (CSDA) range $R_\text{CSDA}$ for a particle which looses energy only through atomic ionization and excitation (neglecting energy losses induced by nuclear reactions), defined as:
\begin{equation}
	R_\text{CSDA}=\int_{E_\text{kin}}^{0}\left(\frac{dE}{dx}\right)^{-1}dE,
	\label{eq:range_csda}
\end{equation}
which is the total distance traveled by a charged particle inside a medium before loosing all its kinetic energy $E_\text{kin}$ and coming to rest. The absorption processes characterizing photon interactions hinder the definition of a photon range, whereas for charge particles the range can have a specific value with small dispersion. This difference directly stems from the fact that photons interactions are governed by absorption, while charged particles mainly interact through energy losses. The diverse beam attenuation for photons and charged particles is displayed in \autoref{fig:beam_atten}.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{beam_atten.pdf}
	\caption[Beam attenuation for photons and charged particles]{Number of particles in a beam as a function of the penetration depth. On the left, absorption processes dominate (typical for photons), which lead to an exponential decrease of the beam intensity. On the right, energy loss in matter (typical for charged particles) leads to a finite range with a fairly local drop to zero in intensity. From \cite{KolanoskiBook}.}
	\label{fig:beam_atten}
\end{figure}
In an ideal situation, the attenuation of charged particles in \autoref{fig:beam_atten} (right) should quickly drop to zero. Conversely, in real-world cases, the distal part of the distribution is smeared by range fluctuations forming an inverse S-shaped edge, a dispersion phenomenon often denominated ``range straggling''. The range straggling is a direct consequence of the energy loss fluctuations discussed in \autoref{ssec:stat_fluct}, and represents a major hazard in PT applications, as detailed in \autoref{ssec:implications_PT}.

To derive a more explicit expression for the charged particle range $R_\text{CSDA}$ in \autoref{eq:range_csda}, it is possible to substitute the kinetic energy $E$ of \autoref{eq:range_csda} with:
\begin{equation}
	E=\left(\gamma-1\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad dE=Mc^2d\gamma.
	\label{eq:differential_kin_en}
\end{equation}
The range can be then expressed as a function of $\gamma_0$, which is the value of $\gamma$ at the entry point of the medium:
\begin{equation}
	R_\text{CSDA}=Mc^2\int_{\gamma_0}^{1}\left\langle\frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle^{-1}d\gamma=\frac{M}{Z^2}f(\gamma_0),
	\label{eq:explicit_range}
\end{equation}
where $f(\gamma_0)$ is a function independent of the mass $M$ and the charge $Z$ of the projectile given by the integration of the Bethe-Bloch equation in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}. Since $R_\text{CSDA}\propto1/Z^2$, heavier particles require more kinetic energy to reach the same depth inside a medium, as shown in \autoref{fig:particle_ranges}.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{particle_ranges.jpg}
	\caption[CSDA range in water for various particles]{CSDA ranges in water as a function of the energy for protons (red line), \ce{^{4}He} (red line), \ce{^{12}C} (blue line), \ce{^{16}O} (green line) and \ce{^{20}Ne} (black line). From \cite{Reidel03177291}.}
	\label{fig:particle_ranges}
\end{figure}
Integrating the \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch} to obtain the $f(\gamma_0)$ in \autoref{eq:explicit_range} is not a trivial task, thus numerical approximation are often employed. A practical range relationship is provided by the Bragg-Kleeman formula \cite{Durante_2016}, which uses a non-relativistic approximation valid in the PT ranges ($E_0\ll2m_ec^2=938.27\,\mathrm{MeV}$):
\begin{equation}
	R_\text{CSDA}=A\left(E_0+\frac{E_0^2}{2m_ec^2}\right)^p\simeq AE_0^p,
	\label{eq:bragg_Kleeman}
\end{equation}
where $E_0=\left(gamma_0-1\right)Mc^2$ is the initial kinetic energy of the projectile particle, and the dimensionless factor $p$ ranges between $1$--$1.8$ in protontherapy and is $1.5$ for low-energy $\alpha$ particles. The \autoref{eq:explicit_range} and \autoref{eq:bragg_Kleeman} are crucial: the strong relationship between range and projectile velocity/kinetic energy allows to decide the depth at which charged particles stop just by tuning a single kinematic property of the projectile (usually the kinetic energy). As \autoref{eq:bragg_Kleeman} is only valid for protons and $\alpha$ particles, scaling relations from \autoref{eq:explicit_range} can be retrieved to find more explicit range formulas applicable to other particles:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{R_2}{R_1}=\frac{M_2}{M_1}\frac{Z_1^2}{Z_2^2},
	\label{eq:range_scaling_different_particles}
\end{equation}
which holds for two charged particles with the same velocity traversing the same material. For instance, the proton range is approximately $3$ times the range of \ce{^{12}C} at the same energy per nucleon, while protons and \ce{^{4}He}-ions with the same velocity have the same range, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:particle_ranges} \cite{Durante_2016}. An additional scaling rule allows to determine the range of the same particles in different media with densities $\rho_{1,2}$ and atomic mass numbers $A_{1,2}$:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{R_2}{R_1}=\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\frac{\sqrt{A_2}}{\sqrt{A_1}}.
	\label{eq:range_scaling_same_particles}
\end{equation}

The longitudinal or range straggling of charged particles shown in \autoref{fig:beam_atten} (right) can be quantitatively estimated using the energy loss distributions discussed in \autoref{ssec:stat_fluct}. In the limit of many collisions, the Gaussian function defined in \autoref{eq:gaussian_dist} can be employed: the energy loss variance $\sigma_{\Delta E}$ can be utilized to calculate the range straggling variance as:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_R=\int\left(\frac{dE}{dx}\right)^{-3}\frac{d\sigma_{\Delta E}}{dx}dE.
	\label{eq:range_variance}
\end{equation}
Solving the \autoref{eq:range_variance} as indicated in \cite{1969PhRv..185..611P}, one finds that the relative range straggling uncertainty of a particle of energy $E$ and mass $M$ is approximately constant:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\sigma_R}{R}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\cdot\phi\left(\frac{E}{Mc^2}\right),
	\label{eq:explicit_range_error}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is a slowly varying function depending on the target material. The $\sigma_R\propto1/\sqrt{M}$ proportionality is extremely intriguing: heavier particles have a smaller relative range straggling because they are less sensitive to each individual ionization process. Thus, longitudinal scattering affects less heavier particles which, consequently, are characterized by a sharper range. The \autoref{eq:explicit_range_error} can be further employed to evaluate scaling laws for different particles with the same range:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\sigma_{R2}}{\sigma_{R1}}=\sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_2}}.
	\label{eq:range_scaling_different_particles_same_range}
\end{equation}
According to equation \autoref{eq:range_scaling_different_particles_same_range}, the relative straggling for \ce{C}-ions is $3.5$ times smaller than for protons. The range straggling and its associated variance need to be kept under control during the treatment planning in PT applications, as detailed in \autoref{ssec:implications_PT}.
\subsection{Bragg peak, SOBP and implications of range straggling on particle therapy}\label{ssec:implications_PT}
To gain a better understanding of the implications on PT induced by inelastic interactions with atomic electrons, it is necessary to take a step back and consider the classical region of the Bethe-Bloch described in \autoref{ssec:bethe_bloch}. Because of the $1/\beta^2$ shape of the Bethe-Bloch curve at low energies (see \autoref{fig:bethe_bloch}), the stopping power surges when the particle velocity decreases. Consequently, at the end of the particle's path much of the energy is lost over a relatively small distance, as shown in \autoref{fig:depth_dose_proton}. In \autoref{fig:bragg_curve_kine}, both the $dE/dx$ and kinetic energy of protons are shown as a function of the penetration depth.

To prove this, let us quantify the Bragg peak width using the range straggling uncertainty $\sigma_R$ in \autoref{eq:explicit_range_error}, as the two quantities are expected to be comparable. According to this equation, at $18\,\mathrm{cm}$ depth the range straggling for a proton is $25\,\mathrm{mm}$, while for \ce{^{12}C} ions it is $7\,\mathrm{mm}$ (calculated from \autoref{eq:range_scaling_different_particles_same_range}). \subsection{Multiple Coulomb scattering}\label{ssec:multiple_coulomb_scattering}
In addition to the inelastic collisions with atomic electrons discussed in \autoref{ssec:bethe_bloch}, charged particles interact electromagnetically with the medium nuclei via Multiple Coulomb Scattering (MCS). As mentioned in the introduction to \autoref{ch:interaction_charged_particles}, the nuclear energy losses of MCS are negligible compared to the ones induced by electrons, due to the comparability between the masses of incoming charged particles and target nuclei. The dominance of electron energy losses over nuclear ones is clearly visible in \autoref{fig:nuclear_energy_losses}.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{nuclear_energy_losses.pdf}
	\caption[Electronic and nuclear $dE/dx$ for \ce{^{12}C} and protons]{Specific energy loss $dE/dx$ of \ce{^{12}C} ions and protons in water. The nuclear energy losses are drawn with a red dashed line. Furthermore, the range of \ce{^{12}C} ions in water corresponding to their specific energy is indicated at the top. From \cite{RevModPhys.82.383}.}
	\label{fig:nuclear_energy_losses}
\end{figure}
The marginal energy transfers to nuclei make the MCS an elastic electromagnetic process, which can be described by the typical Rutherford cross section $d\sigma/d\Omega\propto1/\sin\left({\theta/2}\right)$, given the scattering angle $\theta$. However, the Rutherford formula models the Coulomb scattering off a single nucleus, whereas the MCS can be regarded as a combination of many Rutherford scatterings. This idea was further developed in the Molière theory \cite{passageParticleMatter}, which firstly introduced a detailed description of the MCS, further expanded in \cite{RevModPhys.35.231,RevModPhys.36.881,PhysRevD.20.1584}. Through the Molière theory, it is possible to study MCS more quantitatively through the displacement distribution, which allows to estimate the particle scattering angles produced by MCS deflections. Supposing that an incoming charged particle traverses a thick medium and that the number of nuclear scatterings is very large, the distribution of scattering angles predicted by the Molière theory can be well approximated by a Gaussian distribution (this follows from the application of the central limit theorem). Clearly, the theory of Molière is more complete than the Gaussian approach, allowing to model the rare large-angle scatterings generated by hard collisions that lead to non-Gaussian tails. For the scope of this thesis, the Gaussian approach is presented.

In the small-angle Gaussian approximation, the angular distribution of MCS is specified by the $\theta_{\text{ms}}$ parameter, which is the standard deviation of the Gaussian distribution:
\begin{equation}
	f(\theta_{\text{plane}})\,d\theta_{\text{plane}}
	=
	\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\theta_{\text{ms}}}
	\exp\left(
	-\frac{\theta_{\text{plane}}^2}
	{2\theta_{\text{ms}}^2}
	\right)
	d\theta_{\text{plane}},
	\label{eq:scattering_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_{\text{plane}}$ is the angle projected onto a plane perpendicular to the particle direction of motion, as schematized in \autoref{fig:mcs}. A precise extrapolation of the standard deviation $\theta_{\text{ms}}$ is given by the Highland formula \cite{HIGHLAND1975497}:
\begin{equation}
	\theta_{\text{ms}}
	=
	\frac{13.6\,\mathrm{MeV}/c}{p\beta}
	\,Z
	\sqrt{\frac{x}{X_0}}
	\left(
	1+0.038\ln\frac{x}{X_0}
	\right),
	\label{eq:highland_formula}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the thickness of the scattering material, $X_0\propto A/Z_\text{M}^2$ is the medium's radiation length\footnote{The radiation length $X_0$ is a medium property defined in the context of bremsstrahlung radiation, which is the energy loss through photon irradiation at high energies. Specifically, the bremsstrahlung dominates at energies higher than the critical energy $E_c$ (see \autoref{fig:bethe_bloch}), where the radiative losses overwhelm the excitation/ionization ones. The bremsstrahlung energy losses of an electron with energy $E$ can be modeled as $dE/dx=-E/X_0$.} and $Z$, $p$ and $\beta$ are respectively the incoming particle's charge, momentum and speed. The \autoref{eq:highland_formula} measures the angular displacement $\theta_{\text{ms}}$, however a more informative parameter in MCS is the lateral spatial displacement $y_\text{plane}$ illustrated in \autoref{fig:mcs}, whose approximate dependence on $\theta_{\text{ms}}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
	y_\text{plane}\propto x\theta_{\text{ms}}=\frac{Z}{E_\text{kin}}\cdot
	\sqrt{\frac{x^3}{X_0}},
	\label{eq:lateral_displacement}
\end{equation}
where $E_\text{kin}\simeq\beta c p$ is derived from the classical expression of particles' kinetic energy, and the logarithmic dependence of \autoref{eq:highland_formula} is neglected.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{mcs.pdf}
	\caption[Multiple Coulomb Scattering]{Quantities used to describe the MCS process. The particle, coming from the left, suffers from deflections which deviate its trajectory by an total outgoing angle $\theta_{\text{plane}}$. From \cite{passageParticleMatter}.}
	\label{fig:mcs}
\end{figure}
The $y_\text{plane}$ variable truly provides the main features of MCS, and the way it affects the lateral spread of charged particles, which is often referred to as lateral (or transverse) straggling as opposed to the longitudinal/range straggling defined in \autoref{ssec:range}. First, the lateral scattering increases with the medium thickness and is higher for high atomic number materials, due to the $X_0\propto A/Z_\text{M}^2$ dependence. Second, the lateral straggling decreases with the kinetic energy $E_\text{kin}$ of the particle but increases with its charge $Z$. At the same range, heavier particles have smaller lateral scattering: for example, at $156\,\mathrm{mm}$ in water ($150\,\mathrm{MeV}$ protons versus $285\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ \ce{^{12}C} ions), it can be shown that \cite{Durante_2016}:
\begin{equation}
	\left(\frac{Z}{E_\text{kin}}\right)_{\text{protons}}\simeq3\cdot\frac{Z}{E_\text{kin}}_{\ce{^{12}C}},
	\label{eq:proton_vs_carbon_lateral_scattering}
\end{equation}
meaning that carbon ion have beams $\simeq3$ times narrower than protons, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:lateral_scatterings}, where the beam broadening of various ions is shown. From the picture, it is clear that at the same range heavier particles posses larger beam widths than lighter ones, even if the former have higher charge $Z$. This happens because heavier particles necessitate larger $E_\text{kin}$ than lighter ones to reach the same depth, decreasing the overall beam width as expected from \autoref{eq:lateral_displacement}.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{lateral_scatterings.jpg}
	\caption[Lateral straggling of ions]{Beam width (FWHM, i.e., Full Width at Half Maximum) for proton, helium, and carbon ion beams as a function of the penetration depth. From \cite{radiobiologyTextbook}.}
	\label{fig:lateral_scatterings}
\end{figure}
The heavy beam broadening of protons shown in \autoref{fig:lateral_scatterings} is exploited in a pioneering PT methodology, namely the proton MiniBeam Radio Therapy (MBRT)and wearable detectors.

In conclusion, the physics processes induced by MCS are modeled in Monte Carlo simulations based either on the Molière theory or on the more advanced Lewis theory \cite{osti_4397140}. The latter allows to predict the net displacement, the energy loss and the change of direction at the end of the particle track by simulating discrete single scatterings \cite{rangeVerification}. In addition to Monte Carlo codes, which require high computational resources and are very time consuming, scientists have developed quicker empirical parameterizations to model the large-angle tails of Molière distribution, comprehensively reviewed in \cite{BELLINZONA2015484}.

\section{Nuclear interactions}\label{sec:nucl_interactions}
The electromagnetic interactions described in \autoref{sec:em_interactions} are well understood from both the theoretical and experimental perspectives \cite{10.3389/fonc.2016.00065}, however charged particles can also undergo nuclear interactions with the material nuclei. To interact with the nuclei of matter, charged particles must possess a large energy, sufficiently high to overpass the Coulomb potential barrier generated by atomic electrons and protons. Indeed, nuclear forces have a shorter range ($\mathcal{O}(\text{fm})$) than electromagnetic interactions, and can therefore influence the interaction dynamics only when the projectile and target nuclei are very close to one another. Such spatial proximity unlocks the strong nuclear force, the most intense fundamental force of physics which is responsible for binding the atomic nucleons together. Due to the intervention of the strong force, nuclear interactions often produce vigorous and destructive effects to the interacting projectiles and targets: their nuclear structure can be completely overhauled after the collision due to spallation and break-up processes.

Contrary to electromagnetic interactions, a complete theoretical understanding of nuclear processes is still missing \cite{10.3389/fonc.2016.00065}. Even the most advanced theory of physics interaction, i.e., the Quantum ElectroDynamics (QED), is not capable of performing a detailed description of nuclear interactions, which are many-body problems challenging the current numerical methods at the most fundamental level. Indeed, the particles participating in nuclear interactions are composites (nuclei contain nucleons, which in turn comprise quarks and gluons), and the fundamental theory that describes these interactions is Quantum ChromoDynamics (QCD). Unfortunately, QCD is uniquely calculable in the limit of large momentum transfers, an assumption which does not hold for the relevant energy ranges of nucleus–nucleus collisions in PT and SRP. To describe nuclear interactions without a fundamental theory, scientists have developed many semi-empirical models that are currently embedded in particle transport codes, which are computer programs simulating how radiation and subatomic particles move and interact through matter. Currently, these transport algorithms are efficiently implemented in deterministic software and Monte Carlo simulations \cite{MonteCarloinHeavyChargedParticleTherapy}, which are of great help for understanding the underlying physics processes influencing PT and SRP applications. However, the limited set of available nuclear data \cite{NORBURY2012315} poses great challenges to the development of these models, which need to be continuously benchmarked against experimental data \cite{SIHVER20071320}. The lack of nuclear interaction data is particularly problematic in the PT field: the inherent uncertainty of charged particle transport codes connected to nuclear interactions results in dose-delivery uncertainties leading to tumor under-dosages and healthy tissues over-dosages. Because of these uncertainties, physicians and medical physicists often opt for conservative treatment plannings, where large safety margins are employed around the tumor area. Consequently, these non-optimal procedures may degrade the recognized benefits of charged particle irradiation over conventional photontherapy \cite{rangeVerification}. To circumvent this predicament, the FOOT experiment (detailed in \autoref{ch:foot_experiment}) was devised to fill the existing gaps of nuclear interaction data. One of the principal aims of the FOOT experiment is to bring valuable experimental knowledge to the PT community, thereby making treatment plans predictive rather than just conservative.

Given these premises, let us explore nuclear interactions more in detail. The nuclear processes can be divided into:
\begin{itemize}
	\item elastic collisions, which preserve the total kinetic energy and the nuclear structure of both projectile and target. Nuclear elastic collisions are similar to multiple coulomb scattering (see \autoref{ssec:multiple_coulomb_scattering}), even if the first are induced by the strong force, whereas the second are caused by electromagnetic interactions. The nuclear elastic collisions may either produce weak energy transfers to the target or angular deflections, both simulated with Monte Carlo codes \cite{rangeVerification}. Thus, these interactions may lead to both longitudinal and lateral straggling.
	\item Inelastic collisions, more violent than elastic ones, where the total kinetic energy is not conserved. In nuclear inelastic collisions the projectile might knock out secondary particles (protons, neutrons, deuterons, $\alpha$ particles, etc.) from the target nucleus and break into fragments if it is an heavy ion. Hence, these interactions can lead to multiple effects, namely the production of new particles and/or variations of the internal structure of projectile/target.
\end{itemize}
The probability $P(x)$ that a charged particle does not undergo a nuclear interaction after traveling a distance $x$ in a medium is:
\begin{equation}
	P(x)=\frac{N(x)}{N(0)}=e^{-\frac{x}{\lambda_\text{int}}},
	\label{eq:nucl_int}
\end{equation}
where $N(0)$ and $N(x)$ are respectively the number of incident particles before the medium crossing and after a distance $x$, and $\lambda_\text{int}$ is the interaction length, namely the mean free path of nuclear interactions. $\lambda_\text{int}$ is usually defined as $\lambda_\text{int}=A/(N_A\cdot\sigma_\text{R}\cdot\rho)$, where $\sigma_\text{R}$ is the total nuclear reaction cross section and the remaining parameters are defined in \autoref{tab:bethe_bloch}. The fundamental quantity in \autoref{eq:nucl_int} is the nuclear cross section $\sigma_\text{R}$, which embodies the physics of the reaction. According to \cite{Geant4_NucleusNucleusCrossSections}, $\sigma_\text{R}$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_\text{R}=\sigma_\text{total}-\sigma_\text{elastic},
	\label{eq:nucl_react_def}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\text{total}$ is the total nuclear cross section (which includes both elastic and inelastic processes) and $\sigma_\text{elastic}$ is the cross section for nuclear elastic interactions.

Many experimental and theoretical efforts have been made to correctly estimate nuclear cross sections in order to expand the overall understanding of nuclear interactions. In this regard, a good and simple representation of the nuclear reaction cross section $\sigma_\text{R}$ is given by the Bradt-Peters equation \cite{Bradt1950}:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_\text{R}=\pi R_0^2c_1(E)\left[A_\text{p}^{1/3}+A_\text{t}^{1/3}-c_2(E)\right]^2,
	\label{eq:brad_peters}
\end{equation}
where $A_\text{p}$ and $A_\text{t}$ are respectively the mass numbers of projectile and target, while $R_0$, $c_1$ and $c_2$ are parametrization-specific variables depending on the projectile energy \cite{PhysRevC.11.360,BARSHAY19745}. The meaning of \autoref{eq:brad_peters} is that heavier projectiles and targets increase the nuclear cross section probability, leading to an increment of secondary particle emission. Unfortunately, \autoref{eq:brad_peters} was proved to be valid only for projectile energies $\gtrsim1.5\,\mathrm{GeV/u}$ \cite{ZarrellaPhDThesis}, well above the energy ranges of PT ($100$--$800\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$) and of SRP ($100$--$400\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$). Cross section formulae for the strong interaction at low and intermediate energies are indeed difficult to calculate, as above $1\,\mathrm{GeV}$ the Glauber approximation \cite{SHMAKOV1989125,PhysRevC.67.054607} can be exploited. Nevertheless, in \cite{articleAbuIbrahim}, another nucleus-nucleus total reaction cross section was proposed, valid between $30\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $1\,\mathrm{GeV/u}$:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{R}=\pi C(E) \cdot \left(
	\sqrt{\sigma_{pp}^{\text{tot}} Z_1^{2/3}
	+ \sigma_{pn}^{\text{tot}} N_1^{2/3}}
	+
	\sqrt{\sigma_{pp}^{\text{tot}} Z_2^{2/3}
	+ \sigma_{pn}^{\text{tot}} N_2^{2/3}}
	\right)^2,
	\label{eq:total_nucl_cs}
\end{equation}
where $C(E)$ is an energy-dependent coefficient determined from experimental data, and $Z_{1,2}$ and $N_{1,2}$ represent respectively the proton ($p$) and neutron ($n$) number for the projectile ($1$) and the target ($2$). In addition to \autoref{eq:brad_peters}, \autoref{eq:total_nucl_cs} depends explicitly on the individual nucleon-nucleon cross sections characterizing the overall nucleus-nucleus interaction. The \autoref{eq:total_nucl_cs} was shown to accurately describe Experimental data at lower energies, as shown in \autoref{fig:total_nucl_cs} (middle).
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{total_nucl_cs.jpg}
	\caption[Nuclear cross sections for $p$-\ce{^{12}} and \ce{^{12}}-\ce{^{12}} reactions]{Total nuclear cross sections for $p$-\ce{^{12}} and \ce{^{12}}-\ce{^{12}} reactions expressed as a function of the energy per nucleon $E$. In the top panel, the $\sigma_\text{total}$ (defined in \autoref{eq:nucl_react_def}) for $p$-$p$ and $n$-$p$ collisions are plotted. In the middle panel and bottom panel, experimental data for $p$-\ce{^{12}} and \ce{^{12}}-\ce{^{12}} reactions are shown, respectively. In particular, in the middle panel the $\sigma_{R}$ of \autoref{eq:total_nucl_cs} (solid black line) is shown to accurately describe experimental data. From \cite{articleAbuIbrahim}.}
	\label{fig:total_nucl_cs}
\end{figure}
Since protons and heavier ions' nuclear collisions are modeled in a different way, it is necessary to discuss them separately. As delineated in the following sections, the sole feature that these nuclear interactions have in common is nuclear fragmentation, which represents the most frequent nuclear interaction process at several hundreds MeV per nucleon (the relevant range for PT and SRP). It occurs when projectile and/or target nuclei break apart to form smaller particle pieces, called fragments. Its effects are particularly notable at larger penetration depths, although interaction processes are still dominated by the electromagnetic interactions.
\subsection{Nuclear interactions of protons}\label{ssec:nucl_int_proton}
When a proton hits an atomic nucleus (nucleon-nucleus collision), the collision often initiates a chain of nucleon-nucleon interactions that lead to the emission of protons, neutrons, light fragments and to the equilibration of the remnant nucleus \cite{rangeVerification}. The steps of a proton-nucleus interaction can be described by a three-stage process \cite{Azhgirey_2010,Ferrari:682497}, schematized in \autoref{fig:proton_int_steps}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the first phase is delineated by the Intra-Nuclear Cascade (INC) model, which describes nucleons interactions from $50\,\mathrm{MeV}$ to hundreds of GeV. Despite being firstly proposed in the $1940$s, the INC lies at the basis of modern Monte Carlo codes, and relies on a simple assumption: the incoming nucleon interacts with quasi-free nucleons in the target nucleus through series of two-body interactions. To model the target nucleons in a quasi-free state, the target nucleus must be treated as a Fermi gas. Furthermore, to assume independent nucleon-nucleon interactions, the time between successive collisions must be larger than the duration of a single collision. The INC describes the first $10^{-22}$--$10^{-23}\,\mathrm{s}$ of a proton-nucleon interaction, during which all the secondary particles are produced from the collision, including neutrons, protons and lighter fragments after a coalescence (grouping) mechanism. These secondaries have usually very-high energies and can either scatter again in the same nucleus or simply escape. The comprehensive description of INC process is highly complex because it requires the transport of all secondaries through the nuclear medium, whose properties (e.g., density, quantum effects, nuclear potential, binding energy, etc.) are difficult to parameterize.
	\item The second stage is the pre-equilibrium phase, where the energy of INC particles has lowered to few tens of MeV. Yet, the nucleus is still not capable of reaching thermal equilibrium. The pre-equilibrium stage is usually simulated through the exciton model \cite{PhysRevC.28.1648,PhysRevLett.17.478}, where further coalescence processes and nucleon-nucleon collisions lead to the emission of neutrons, protons and light fragments, ultimately leaving the nucleus in an equilibrium state with a certain amount excitation energy. The first two stages compose the so-called ``dynamic'' part of the overall process.
	\item The last stage is the de-excitation step, usually slower than the first two (it takes $10^{-18}$--$10^{-16}\,\mathrm{s}$) where the target nucleus finally dissipates its energy through four possible ways:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item nuclear evaporation, where light fragments are successively emitted from the excited nucleus similarly to an evaporation from a hot system;
		\item fission, where the excited nucleus ($Z\gtrsim65$) is split in two fragments (not relevant for PT);
		\item Fermi-breakup, where low-$Z$ nuclei ($A\lesssim16$) disassemble in one step into smaller fragments;
		\item gamma-ray ($\gamma$) emission.
	\end{itemize}
	Due to its slowness, the third step is usually referred to as the ``slow'' part of the overall process.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Nuclear interactions of heavy ions}\label{ssec:nucl_int_ions}
When an heavy ion hits a nucleus (nucleus-nucleus collision), the interaction is endowed with more degrees of freedom than a simpler proton-nucleus collision, as the heavy ion is constituted of bounded nucleons with an interconnected dynamic. This makes heavy ions collisions inevitably more unpredictable than proton-nucleus interactions, with a wider phenomenology and increased randomness. For example, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:proton_ion_fragm} (bottom), heavy ion interactions lead to both projectile and target fragmentation, in contrast to protons which can only produce target-like fragments.

	\centering

	\caption[Fragment emission in nucleon-nucleus and nucleus-nucleus interactions]{Top: emission of a target fragment after a proton-nucleus interaction. Bottom: projectile-like and target-like fragments ejected after a nucleus-nucleus collision. From \cite{rangeVerification}.}
	\label{fig:proton_ion_fragm}
\end{figure}




In \autoref{ssec:implications_PT} and \autoref{ssec:implications_PT2}, it was stated that the fluctuations induced by longitudinal and lateral straggling are lower for heavier ions compared to protons. Despite this, nuclear interactions are somehow more impactful on heavy ions than on protons, especially regarding the fragmentation processes. This can be noted in \autoref{fig:proton_carbon_absolute}, which shows a comparison of dose-averaged Linear Energy Transfer (LET) between protons and carbon ions in water with the same range. The concept of LET will be specified in (?? sec LET), but for the moment it can be viewed simply as the analogous of the energy loss per unit path length $dE/dx$. Although the common practice in literature is to normalize different Bragg curves either to have the same area or to posses the same peak height (as shown in \autoref{fig:proton_carbon_width}), in \autoref{fig:proton_carbon_absolute} the Bragg functions are shown without normalization. This allows us to point out several features regarding the different profile distribution of protons and carbons. First, \ce{^{12}C} releases more energy than the proton along all the particles' range, an effect which is due to the quadratic dependence $Z^2$ of \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}. Second, after the Bragg peak, carbon ions release a much larger dose than protons, visible from the heavy tail located after the distal fall-off of the Bragg region. This unique feature is valid for all the ions heavier than protons, and can be explained only through nuclear fragmentation induced by heavy ions, which generates not only target fragments (as proton-nucleus collisions), but also projectile fragments. As projectile fragments originate from the break-up of the incoming nucleus, they have an atomic number $Z$ lower than that of the fissioning nucleus, but nearly the same direction, kinetic energy per nucleon and, consequently, the same $\beta$. Hence, according to the Bethe-Bloch formula in \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch}, they dissipate a lower amount of energy per unit length compared to the primary, traversing longer distances. Similar conclusions can be drawn analyzing \autoref{eq:range_scaling_different_particles}: lower-$Z$ fragments would have a larger range than the primary particle from which they originate. Both these arguments clearly explain the downstream tail of heavy ions, visible also in \autoref{fig:proton_carbon_width} and \autoref{fig:bragg_peaks_carbon_energies} Bragg curves.

Indeed, as the penetration depth of carbon ions increases, the peak-to-entrance dose ratio diminishes dramatically from $\simeq7$ at $200\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ to $3.4$ at $400\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$. This proves that the number of primary \ce{^{12}C} decreases with the range as a consequence of fragmentation. The loss of beam fluence strictly depends on the inelastic nuclear cross section in \autoref{eq:nucl_int} and varies sharply from particle to particle. For example, at the range of $16\,\mathrm{cm}$, nearly $20\%$ of $160\,\mathrm{MeV}$ protons would be lost due to nuclear reactions, whereas approximately $50\%$ of $290\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ carbons would undergo nuclear reactions before reaching the same distance \cite{rangeVerification}. In addition to this, in \autoref{fig:bragg_peaks_carbon_energies} it is possible to observe the broadening of the Bragg peak width with the penetration depth. This effect, expected from \autoref{eq:explicit_range_error}, is caused by the range fluctuations induced by both the primary and its fragments.

The nuclear fragmentation effects are even more pronounced in ions heavier than \ce{^{12}C}. For example, \autoref{fig:heavy_fragm} illustrates the fragment dose contributions to a $670\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ \ce{^{20}Ne} beam with a range of $36\,\mathrm{cm}$ in water. For this reason, no beams heavier than \ce{^{16}O} are effectively employed in clinical practice. Clearly, the study of nuclear fragmentation for ions more massive than \ce{^{16}O} should not be discarded. \subsection{Monte Carlo codes for nuclear interactions}\label{ssec:monte_carlo_codes}
The challenging aspect of \autoref{eq:brad_peters} and \autoref{eq:total_nucl_cs} is that they provide no information regarding the angle of emission or the kinetic energy of the possible fragments produced during nuclear interactions, limiting the comprehension of fragmentation processes. Indeed, the dynamic of nuclear fragments cannot be characterized by a comprehensive and rigorous theoretical framework, but rather by microscopic models implemented in Monte Carlo simulations. Two of the most frequently used Monte Carlo codes for PT and SRP studies are FLUKA and Geant4. Both these tools allow to emulate the transport of particles inside any material and their consequent interactions and biological impact. Combining the available experimental data with the simulations generated by FLUKA and Geant4 allows to update the databases of programs like TRiP98 \cite{KRAMER2009989} and SpaceTRiP \cite{Luoni:2023tgo}, representing the two gold standard codes for treatment planning and space radiation protection, respectively. In the following paragraphs, a review of their most relevant features is briefly outlined.
\subsubsection{FLUKA}\label{sssec:fluka}
The FLUKA (FLUktuierende KAskade) simulation tool \cite{Ferrari:2005zk,BOHLEN2014211} is a Monte Carlo code generator written in FORTRAN used to simulate particles' transport and interactions in matter from few keV to cosmic ray energies. Nowadays, it is widely employed in shielding applications (relevant for SRP) and medical physics.

In FLUKA, charged particles are transported through a multiple coulomb scattering algorithm, based on the advanced Molière theory in \cite{FERRARI1992412}, and on the statistical approach in \cite{Rinaldi_2011,articleFasso} that models the ionization energy losses induced by electrons, including energy fluctuations and $\delta$ ray emission. In addition, FLUKA employs the PEANUT (Pre-Equilibrium Approach to NUclear Thermalization) model \cite{Ferrari:682497,osti_21055002} to simulate hadron-nucleus interactions up to few tens of TeV, including the first two stages of proton-nucleus interactions described in \autoref{ssec:nucl_int_proton}. The cross sections loaded in FLUKA are based on parameterized fits and, when available, they are sourced from tabulated datasets, otherwise they are evaluated with appropriate nuclear models, such as ENDF \cite{WILLIAMS1991549} and JENDL \cite{KOSTAL2012275} for neutron transport.

To simulate the dynamic stage of nucleus-nucleus interactions between $100\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ and $5\,\mathrm{GeV/u}$ a relativistic Quantum Molecular Dynamics (rQMD) model is used \cite{ANDERSEN20041302}, while below $100\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ the BME \cite{Cerutti2006LOWEN} approach is employed. FLUKA algorithms are then able to connect the dynamic stage simulation to the slow one, including evaporation, fission, beak-up and gamma emission processes.
\subsubsection{Geant4}\label{sssec:geant4}
Geant4 \cite{Geant4Paper} is a Monte Carlo simulation toolkit written in C++ able to simulate the passage of particles in matter. Despite being originally developed for the high-energy physics at LHC, it has been adapted also to medical physics and space science.

In Geant4, the multiple coulomb scattering of charged particles is simulated through the Lewis theory \cite{osti_4397140}, while the energy losses are modeled through the Bethe-Bloch formula combined with low-energy parametrizations. Statistical tools are included to simulate the range straggling of charged particles.

Regarding the hadronic interactions, Geant4 offers a wide selection of nuclear models. Starting with protons, the dynamic part of inelastic nuclear interactions can be simulated with the Binary Cascade Model (BIC) \cite{Folger2004-pz}, which models the INC stage with nuclear potential effects derived from quantum mechanics, similarly to FLUKA. In addition to BIC, Geant4 provides the intra-nuclear cascade Liège (INCL) \cite{PhysRevC.90.054602} model, which differs from the former under many aspects, namely the nuclear potential treatment, the nuclear density and the coalescence mechanisms. The Geant4 toolkit is able to accurately describe the proton de-excitation step using standard evaporation models like the Weisskopf-Ewing approach \cite{Dostrovsky1959-na} for the emission of light fragments up to \ce{^{4}He}, or more advanced ones, such as the generalized evaporation model, which is capable of simulating the emission of heavier fragments. To evaluate nuclear cross sections, Geant4 can either calculate them (using the ENDF model like FLUKA) or search them in nuclear databases. To simulate heavier particles' interactions, Geant4 uses the G4BinaryLightIonReaction (BLI) model, an INC model extended to consider individual nucleon interactions in the calculation. In addition, Geant4 can include QMD calculations in nucleus-nucleus interactions by means of the G4QMDReaction model. Alternatively, the dynamic stage of heavy ion interactions can be simulated using the INCL++ model. The de-excitation phase of heavy ion collisions is instead described by the aforementioned de-excitation models.
