\chapter{Detector optimization and momentum reconstruction}\label{ch:analysis_vt_msd_momentum}
As detailed in \autoref{ch:foot_experiment}, the final goal of the FOOT Collaboration is achieving a very high resolution on nuclear fragmentation cross section measurements. To fulfill this aim, it is necessary to constantly evaluate the subdetector performances and to keep the event reconstruction efficiency under control. For this reason, along with the hardware work presented in \autoref{ch:trigger_board}, during my thesis I contributed to upgrading the FOOT tracking system, which provides the global reconstruction of primary and secondary tracks traversing the electronic setup (see \autoref{sec:electronic_setup}). Specifically, the tracking system analysis presented in this chapter considers experimental data collected at the CNAO 2025 campaign, where the FOOT Collaboration employed a $200\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$ carbon beam to irradiate a $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ \ce{C} target for studying nuclear fragmentation. In addition, some of the data runs I analyzed did not foresee the magnetic field, which was rightly deactivated for alignment and calibration purposes; the absence of the magnetic field in a given run will be specified throughout the text.

\section{Global track reconstruction with SHOE}\label{sec:shoe}
To process, reconstruct and analyze the experimental data acquired by FOOT, the Collaboration has developed the SHOE (Software for Hadrontherapy Optimization Experiment) framework \cite{shoeFramework}, a \texttt{C++} object-oriented software based on the ROOT libraries \cite{ROOT_framework} partly inherited from the FIRST \cite{PhysRevC.93.064601} experiment. The SHOE workflow comprises all the reconstruction steps, from the collection of subdetector raw data to the evaluation of cross sections, as schematized in \autoref{fig:shoe_workflow}. The picture shows that the raw experimental data acquired by the TDAQ system and packed according to \autoref{fig:data_fragment} is firstly processed by detector-specific libraries during the local reconstruction phase (see \autoref{ssec:local_reconstruction}), and is successively assembled in the global reconstruction stage (see \autoref{ssec:global_reconstruction}), which saves the data structures using the ROOT format. From the globally reconstructed tracks it is finally possible to extrapolate all the relevant physics quantities (charge, momentum, kinetic energy, etc.) in order to perform the identification of fragments and, consequently, the cross section measurements.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{shoe_workflow.pdf}
	\caption[SHOE workflow]{Steps of the SHOE workflow. Details are reported in the text. Adapted from \cite{UbaldiPhDThesis}.}
	\label{fig:shoe_workflow}
\end{figure}
The SHOE framework has also been conceived to generate the Monte Carlo simulations needed to emulate the particle transport and interactions through the FOOT electronic setup. Such feature allows to directly test the various nuclear models implemented in Monte Carlo programs and to compare them with real-world data; moreover, it contributes to calculate the critical efficiency term $\varepsilon$ in \autoref{eq:cross_section_single_differential}. To recreate the real experimental conditions and resolutions, SHOE replicates the detector geometries and materials employing the FLUKA and Geant4 codes described in \autoref{ssec:monte_carlo_codes}, which allow to reproduce a true ``digital twin'' of the experiment. In particular, the FLUKA software is configured to produce a event-by-event simulation that enables to precisely evaluate the fragments' simulated properties.

To manage both the experimental and simulated data with the correct magnetic field maps and the right detector configuration, geometry and calibration, a SHOE campaign manager combines the configuration parameters (specific of each run) to the processed data events, as schematized in \autoref{fig:shoe_workflow}.
\subsection{Local reconstruction}\label{ssec:local_reconstruction}
The purpose of the local reconstruction phase is to create a decoded version of the raw experimental data acquired by the FOOT TDAQ system. In this initial stage, a SHOE reader process uses the headers of data fragments and full events (see \autoref{fig:data_fragment}) to associate the detector-specific libraries with the $4$-byte data words generated by the passage of a particle. This fundamental action allows to translate encoded bit information into meaningful physics quantities such as times, local positions in each subdetector's frame of reference, pixel/strip cluster information like size and deposited energy, etc.

Contrary to experimental data, Monte Carlo simulated events require a digitization-like procedure that mimics the real detector response. For instance, when a real particle traverses a VT plane, it fires a cluster of several pixels rather than activating a single one. The cluster shape and dimension are governed by the width of the diffusion cloud (dependent on the deposited energy), the inclination of the particle direction and the threshold level on the pixel discriminators. To replicate the creation of a VT cluster also in simulations, SHOE implements a special parametrization that builds the cluster size using a function inspired from \cite{REIDEL2021165807}, which correlates the number of fired pixels to the particle's deposited energy. In addition, simulations can also model the VT cluster shape by employing a spiraling algorithm, which distributes the released energy among neighboring pixels starting from the central pixel and progressively extending outwards.

Although the local reconstruction works differently between experimental and simulated data, the final decoded output possesses a similar format, guaranteeing that the subsequent analysis procedure can be applied in both cases within the same SHOE software.

Among the various processes performed in the local reconstruction stage, the most important tasks are the ToF Wall (TW) charge identification and the vertex tracking, which are briefly described in the following paragraphs.
\subsubsection{ToF Wall charge reconstruction}\label{sssec:tw_charge_reco}
As already mentioned in the introduction to \autoref{sec:electronic_setup}, the TW charge reconstruction method is used to assign the correct atomic number (i.e., the charge) to the particles traversing the FOOT electronic setup. According to \autoref{eq:bethe_bloch} and \autoref{eq:beta}, the energy deposited in a detector (in this case, the TW scintillating bars) by a charged particle, its Time of Flight (ToF) and its charge $Z$ are inherently connected to the particle's velocity $\beta$; their relationship can be better observed from the $\Delta E$-ToF plot illustrated in \autoref{fig:charge_mass_id}.

To reconstruct the particle $Z$, it is first necessary to measure the $\Delta E$ and the ToF of each TW point, which define a point in the $\Delta E$-ToF plot. Successively, through a dichotomic algorithm, the $\Delta E$-ToF point is matched with the closest Bethe-Bloch curve (black solid lines in \autoref{fig:changed_curv} (left)) that, being parameterized by $Z$, provides the final particle charge.
\subsubsection{Vertex tracking}\label{sssec:vertex_tracking}
In \autoref{sssec:vertex_tracker}, it was stated that one of the critical tasks of the VT detector is to reconstruct the interaction point between the primary ion beam and the target, hereafter referred to as VT point. Identifying the position of the VT point is crucial, as it allows to reconstruct the origin point of nuclear fragments generated in the beam-target collision. To reconstruct the VT point in each FOOT event (represented by a primary ion beam traversing the subdetectors), the vertex clustering algorithm \cite{RESCIGNO201434} needs the positions of all the pixel clusters fired in the four VT planes, as schematized by the event display in \autoref{fig:vertex_tracking}. Successively, for each event, the algorithm selects clusters located in the plane furthest from the target, as the cluster density is lower downstream. Then, the algorithm moves backwards building all the possible combinations between the furthest clusters and the one belonging to the previous layers, generating a preliminary 2D track representation fitting the clusters from which it was created. Once the plane closest to the target is reached, all the input cluster points are included, however a track can be validated only if it includes at least three VT clusters and it observes a $\chi^2$ quality criterion. A track validation automatically prevents the associated clusters from being further inspected in successive track finding iteration attempts.

When all the tracks of a FOOT event are reconstructed, it is possible to build the $\left(x,y,z\right)$ coordinates of the VT point, the first two labeling the transverse position and the latter the parallel one with respect to the beam direction. First, the reconstructed tracks are extrapolated onto the VT planes; then, their centroid is calculated, and the resulting $\left(x,y\right)$ pair defines the VT point transverse coordinates. The $z$ coordinate is instead computed minimizing the average track distances from the centroid position, always employing a binary search algorithm implemented in \cite{RESCIGNO201434}.

\subsection{Global reconstruction}\label{ssec:global_reconstruction}
The global reconstruction phase assembles the independent, detector-specific physical quantities built upon the local subdetector hits into a coherent global track, extending from the VT point to the downstream region of the electronic setup. FOOT global tracks do not merely represent the particle trajectory across the whole experimental setup, but they also incorporate the particle state, which encapsulates relevant physics quantities at each track point. The details of the global reconstruction algorithm are provided in the following paragraphs, which describe the GENFIT toolkit and the Kalman Filter algorithm.
\subsubsection{GENFIT toolkit and the Kalman Filter algorithm}\label{sssec:genfit_kalman_filter}
GENFIT \cite{Rauch_2015} is an open-source, experiment-independent \texttt{C++} tracking package employed by SHOE to perform the global reconstruction phase. Similarly to SHOE, GENFIT is integrated with the ROOT and Geant4 frameworks, enabling a consistent treatment of experimental and simulated particle tracks. To correctly employ GENFIT, a SHOE uploader program converts the detector information into GENFIT-compatible measurements which conform to the diverse technologies of the FOOT subdetectors. The GENFIT working principle can be divided in three main stages \cite{UbaldiPhDThesis}, namely measurement, track representation and track fitting, which are described in the following.
\subsubsection{Measurement phase}\label{sssec:meas_phase}
In the measurement phase the SHOE-based observables acquired from every detector are collected by GENFIT, along with their respective uncertainties. In this stage, GENFIT converts the subdetector information into predefined \texttt{C++} classes that are coherent to the specific detector technology (pixels, strips, wires, etc.).
\subsubsection{Track representation phase}\label{sssec:trk_rep_phase}
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{track_param.jpg}
	\caption[GENFIT track parametrization]{Visualization of a track parametrization in GENFIT. From \cite{ZarrellaPhDThesis}.}
	\label{fig:track_param}
\end{figure}
In the track representation phase GENFIT defines a set of univocal parameters that represent the state of a particle track, from which it is possible to extrapolate the particle's local position. GENFIT calculates such parameters and their covariance at any given point of the particle trajectory. To build a track representation and extrapolate the track parameters, GENFIT employs the Runge-Kutta-Nysrt{\"o}n (RKN) numerical integration method \cite{Rauch_2015} to calculate the solution to the equation of motion describing a charged particle immersed in a magnetic field:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{d^2 \vec{r}}{ds^2}
	=
	\frac{q}{p}
	\left(
	\frac{d\vec{r}}{ds}
	\times
	\vec{B}(\vec{r})
	\right),
	\label{eq:track_motion}
\end{equation}
where $\vec{r}$ is the particle position vector, $s$ is the curvilinear coordinate along the track, $q$ and $p$ are respectively the charge and momentum of the particle and $\vec{B}(\vec{r})$ is the magnetic field. As the FOOT magnetic field is non-uniform, this numerical approach is preferred to more challenging analytical methods. Finally, a GENFIT track representation is encapsulated by a $5$-dimensional vector $\vect{x}$ defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
	\vect{x} = \left(q/p,\, u',\, v',\, u,\, v\right),
	\label{eq:track_representation}
\end{equation}
with:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		u' &= \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \hat{u}}{\vec{a} \cdot\hat{n}},\\
		v' &= \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \hat{v}}{\vec{a} \cdot \hat{n}}, \\
		u  &= (\vec{r} - \vec{o}) \cdot \hat{u}, \\
		v  &= (\vec{r} - \vec{o}) \cdot \hat{v},
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $q/p$ is the charge-over-momentum ratio (inverse of rigidity), $u$ and $v$ are the particle coordinates in the local reference frame of a detector plane with origin $\vec{o}$, $u'$ and $v'$ describe the track direction with respect to the plane, and $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{a}$ are respectively the global track position and direction. A visual representation of these vectors is shown in \autoref{fig:track_param}.
\subsubsection{Track fitting phase}\label{sssec:trk_fit_phase}
In the track fitting stage, the measurement and the track representation phases are used to determine the most probable particle trajectory. Among the various track fitting algorithms available in GENFIT, the FOOT Collaboration decided to implement a Kalman Filter, which possess multiple advantages when compared to other methods like Helix, Pline or least-squares \cite{Rotondi2009Kalman}. First, Kalman Filters can perform particle tracking in inhomogeneous magnetic field, including energy losses and multiple scattering events. Second, the Kalman Filter is a recursive fitting algorithm that progressively updates the track parameters as new measurements become available. In practice, each iteration just requires the results from the previous steps, with no necessity of performing the whole fit each time a new measurement is added. This allows to closely follow the physical trajectory of the track, rather than extrapolating the particle trail from the initial conditions as in a global fit \cite{slide_arcelli}. To achieve this, the Kalman Filter iteratively combines the detector measurements with the predicted track's trajectory to obtain the best estimate of the particle state and its uncertainties. While a detailed description of the FOOT Kalman Filter algorithm is provided in \cite{ZarrellaPhDThesis}, its operation can be summarized in three main processes, namely prediction, filtering and smoothing. The predictive stage propagates the track state vector $\vect{x}$ from the detector plane $k-1$ to the following one, generating a predicted state vector $\tilde{\vect{x}}_k$ with covariance:
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\matr{C}}_k = \matr{J}\, \matr{C}_{k-1}\, \transp{\matr{J}} + \matr{N},
	\label{eq:cov_mat_pred}
\end{equation}
composed of a Jacobian term $\matr{J}$ describing error propagation and a noise matrix $\matr{N}$ accounting for multiple scattering, energy losses and other propagation uncertainties. When a measurement $\vect{m}_k$ at detector plane $k$ is available, the filtering phase begins, where the residual $\vect{r}_k$ between the prediction and the actual measurement can be computed:
\begin{equation}
	\vect{r}_k = \vect{m}_k - \matr{H}_k\, \tilde{\vect{x}}_k,
	\label{eq:residual_pred}
\end{equation}
where the matrix $\matr{H}_k$ converts the state vector into measurable detector coordinates. Finally, the Kalman gain $\matr{K}_k$ (obtained through a combination of the covariance matrices of both the prediction and the measurement) determines how much the measurement should influence the updated state by comparing their uncertainties and trusting more the lower one. The updated state finally becomes:
\begin{equation}
	\vect{x}_k = \tilde{\vect{x}}_k + \matr{K}_k\, \vect{r}_k.
	\label{eq:updated_state}
\end{equation}
The prediction and filtering steps proceed iteratively up to the last detection layer, continuously refining the estimate of the track state vector, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:kalman_filter}.

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{kalman_filter.jpg}

In the successive steps, the track is extrapolated to the next detector planes and the fit is corrected taking into accounts the real position measurements (blue stars) and their uncertainties. The process is repeated for all the detector planes until the final track representation is obtained. From \cite{Kisel_2014}.}

\end{figure}\noindent
\autoref{fig:kalman_filter} also shows that the precision of the track state vector increases with the traversed path, as downstream detector planes contain the information measured from the previous layers. This introduces a bias in the Kalman Filter: one should prefer running the algorithm from the downstream detectors to the upstream region, guaranteeing that the best position estimates are obtained close to the VT detector, where the cluster and truck densities are the highest. To circumvent this inequality, the the Kalman Filter must foresee a final smoothing stage. In FOOT this is achieved by applying the track fitting in the forward (from the VT to the TW) and backward direction (from the TW to the VT) for $10$ times until a p-value convergence criterion is met. The final Kalman Filter parameters are then given by the weighted average of the forward and backward Kalman Filters, increasing the procedure robustness. A brief description of the forward Kalman Filter is provided in the caption of \autoref{fig:forward_kalman_filter}; a detailed explanation of the backward Filter (also named ``backtracking'' algorithm) is instead reported in \cite{ZarrellaPhDThesis}.

\section{Optimization of the Vertex Tracker}\label{sec:optim_vt}
This section presents my initial contributions to the SHOE tracking software, conducted under the supervision of the FOOT Bologna group. Specifically, my analysis work started off by studying the alignment issues of the MIMOSA-28 VT detector (see \autoref{sssec:vertex_tracker}), the one currently employed in the electronic setup.\footnote{The following analysis does not include the new VT presented in \autoref{sec:new_vertex_tracker}, which will be operated as of November 2026.} To describe my alignment studies, it is essential to introduce the concept of the pulls.
\subsection{Pull distributions}\label{ssec:pull_distributions}
The pull distribution are useful statistical tools that compare the detector-measured coordinates $\vect{x}_\text{meas}=(x_\text{meas},y_\text{meas})$ with the GENFIT-fitted coordinates $(x_\text{fit},y_\text{fit})$ in the transverse direction of the beam. The $\text{pull}_x$ and $\text{pull}_y$ for a given detector plane are dimensionless quantities defined as:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\text{pull}_x &=
		\frac{x_{\text{meas}}-x_{\text{fit}}}
		{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{x,\text{meas}}-\sigma^2_{x,\text{fit}}}},
		&
		\text{pull}_y &=
		\frac{y_{\text{meas}}-y_{\text{fit}}}
		{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{y,\text{meas}}-\sigma^2_{y,\text{fit}}}}
		\quad \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:pull_distributions}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\text{meas}$ represents the hit/cluster uncertainty coming from the detector measurement and $\sigma_\text{fit}$ is the position error built from the covariance matrix in \autoref{eq:cov_mat_pred}, which is recursively obtained through the GENFIT Kalman Filter algorithm.

According to \cite{pullArticle}, for unbiased $\vect{x}_\text{meas}$ and $\sigma_\text{meas}$, the pull distributions follow a standard normal distribution, as indicated in \autoref{eq:pull_distributions}, i.e., $(\mu_\text{pull},\sigma_\text{pull})\simeq(0,1)$. This peculiar property renders pull distributions relevant diagnostic tools for the measurement chain. On one hand, a pull mean $\mu_\text{pull}\simeq0$ implies that the position residual $\vect{x}_{\text{meas}}-\vect{x}_{\text{fit}}\simeq0$, meaning that the $z$ coordinate of the detection plane is perfectly aligned, producing no systematic shifts along the reconstructed transverse coordinates. Practically, this means that the real detector position corresponds to the one uploaded in the GENFIT libraries. On the other hand, if the pull width $\sigma_\text{pull}\simeq1$, the uncertainty model correctly estimates the ``true'' measurement error.

Another interesting property of the pull definition is the denominator, which at first glance might look surprising due to the presence of a minus sign. Actually, the pull denominator is simply the residual error computed propagating the $\sigma_\text{meas}$ and $\sigma_\text{fit}$ uncertainties:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma^2_\text{residual}=\sigma^2_\text{meas}+\sigma^2_\text{fit}-2\sigma_\text{meas,fit},
	\label{eq:residual_err}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\text{meas,fit}$ is the covariance term, which takes into account the correlation between the measurement and fit errors. Indeed, $\sigma_\text{meas}$ and $\sigma_\text{fit}$ are correlated because of the $\matr{K}_k$ mentioned in \autoref{sssec:trk_fit_phase}, which is used in \autoref{eq:updated_state} to calculate the updated track state vector $\vect{x}$, defined in \autoref{eq:track_representation}. In other words, the correlation stems from the fact that the Kalman Filter not only compares its predictions with the measurements, but also their uncertainties, trusting more the preciser quantity. The fact that measurements are used to evaluate the final fit position is called ``self-bias'' in the statistical jargon. Similarly to $\vect{x}_{\text{meas}}$ and $\vect{x}_{\text{fit}}$, also $\sigma_\text{meas}$ and $\sigma_\text{fit}$ depend on each other, and their squared difference sort of removes the common fluctuation affecting the residual error.

The study of pull distributions is not merely a technical detail specific to the alignment problem or to the evaluation of an uncertainty model. The pull definition, indeed, associates them to $\chi^2$ distributions:
\begin{equation}
	\chi^2\approx\sum\text{pull}.
	\label{eq:pull_chi_squared_dist}
\end{equation}
Despite not being a straightforward relationship, \autoref{eq:pull_chi_squared_dist} states that the validity of $\chi^2$ tests is based on pull distributions. Specifically, $\chi^2$ selection cuts are fundamental to improve the track reconstruction performances (which aim for a momentum accuracy of $2$--$3\%$) and to reduce the background contamination. This reasoning profoundly motivates the study of pull distributions, which will be extensively treated in this section and also in \autoref{sec:optim_msd}. The expected result of this study is that the VT and MSD optimization will improve the momentum reconstruction analysis carried out in \autoref{sec:momentum_reco} (??).

The observation on the pull distributions prompted a more in-depth investigation of the alignment issue. To this regard, I decided to develop a $\chi^2$ minimization procedure to estimate the longitudinal ($z$) displacement of VT planes directly from data, measuring the discrepancy between expected and actual planes' position. The \autoref{fig:chi_squared_alignment_algo} illustrates the basic idea of the implemented $\chi^2$ algorithm. Without loss of generality, the picture only shows the $x$-$z$ coordinate plane, however the algorithm was implemented also in the $y$-$z$ plane. The initial assumption is that the detector plane is located at $z_0$ (in other words, this is the position GENFIT uploads from the configuration parameters). If it were perfectly aligned, a particle following the blue track line would hit it exactly at the green dot, producing the transverse measurement $x_0$. However, a hit is recorded at the red dot $x_M$, which is higher than expected ($x_M>x_0$). As the track has a positive slope $t_x=dx/dz>0$, the $x$ coordinate of its position increases with $z$. Having measured a larger $x$ value than expected, the particle must have traveled further along the $z$ axis before hitting the detector plane, meaning that the physical plane is actually shifted to the right of where GENFIT thought it was ($z_\text{ideal}>z_0$). By displacing the detector layer to the right amount $\Delta z\equiv z_\text{ideal}-z_0$, the discrepancy between $x_M$ and $x_0$ would zero.

Ultimately, the aim of the $\chi^2$ minimization algorithm is to estimate the longitudinal displacement $\Delta z$, which induces the non-zero position residuals in the transverse $x$-$y$ coordinates. The track of a particle can be parameterized as a straight line $\mathbf{l}$ in space (no magnetic field can influence the VT tracklets), with $(x_0, y_0)$ being the fitted-track point at $z_0$ and $(t_x, t_y)$ the track inclination:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{l}:
	\begin{cases}
		x = x_0 + t_x\,(z - z_0), \\
		y = y_0 + t_y\,(z - z_0).
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:track_parametrization}
\end{equation}
Let $(x_M, y_M)$ be the measured hit position and $(\sigma_x,\sigma_y)$ the corresponding spatial resolutions. If the plane was located at a generic longitudinal position $z$, the spatial discrepancy between measured and expected hit is quantified by:
\begin{equation}
	\chi^2(z) =
	\frac{\bigl(x_M - x_0 - t_x\,(z - z_0)\bigr)^2}{\sigma_x^2}
	+
	\frac{\bigl(y_M - y_0 - t_y\,(z - z_0)\bigr)^2}{\sigma_y^2}.
	\label{eq:chi2_z}
\end{equation}
The ideal plane position $z_\text{ideal}$ is the value of $z$ that minimizes the following quantity:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{d\chi^2}{dz}\,(z) = 0.
	\label{eq:chi2_minimization}
\end{equation}
Solving \autoref{eq:chi2_minimization} for $z$ yields:
\begin{equation}
	z_\text{ideal} = z_0 +
	\frac{\dfrac{t_x}{\sigma_x^2}\,(x_M - x_0) + \dfrac{t_y}{\sigma_y^2}\,(y_M - y_0)}
	{\dfrac{t_x^2}{\sigma_x^2} + \dfrac{t_y^2}{\sigma_y^2}},
	\label{eq:z_ideal}
\end{equation}
which finally gives:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta z\equiv z_\text{ideal} - z_0.
	\label{eq:delta_z}
\end{equation}
Following the $\Delta z$ analysis, the FOOT Bologna group measured that the GENFIT geometry parameters of VT1 were not matching its real position. The subsequent displacement correction significantly improved the estimated $\Delta z$, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:delta_z_improv}, which shows the $\Delta z$ distributions for the helium fragment and their approximate Gaussian fit. The correction brings the $\Delta z$ from $323\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to $75.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (extrapolated from the fit), validating the misalignment suggested by the non-zero pull means.

\subsubsection{Software-based alignment procedure}\label{sssec:software_based_alignment_procedure}
Previously, it was stated that the VT1 geometric correction effectively reduced the pull mean displacement. However, \autoref{fig:postZ_pullSummaryVT} showed that the pulls were still not centered in $0$, as expected. To address this issue, it is important to consider that any variation in GENFIT geometry parameters must be followed by a new software-based alignment procedure. In other words, after the change in the VT1 geometric parameters, a new realignment of the whole electronic setup must be performed. This software-based alignment uses an iterative algorithm to minimize the difference between the real beam position and the one measured by each detector plane. Then, the algorithm not only adjusts the position of each subdetector (inter-detector alignment), but calibrates the position of each plane (inter-plane alignment). In practice, the global reconstruction (see \autoref{ssec:global_reconstruction}) requires this procedure to match the electronic setup geometry with the one of its ``digital twin'' built through the GENFIT libraries.

To perform the software-based alignment, a detector plane is firstly chosen as reference. Then, a coordinate transformation matrix dynamically adjusts the $x$-$y$ position of each detector plane relative to the chosen reference detector, minimizing the residual on the beam position. Finding the root cause of the $\sigma_\text{pull}$ displacement from $0$ represents a difficult challenge. Nonetheless, the following discussion can help us formulating hypothesis that are plausibly correlated to the $\sigma_\text{pull}$ issues.

As stated in \autoref{ssec:pull_distributions}, a pull width larger than one indicates that the uncertainties assigned to the measurements from which it is computed are underestimated. Observing \autoref{eq:pull_distributions}, this means that the actual fluctuations of the residuals are larger than expected from the error model currently used in the reconstruction.

For the VT, the cluster position is reconstructed using a Center-of-Gravity (CoG) computation over the pixels belonging to the cluster. The cluster coordinates $(x_{\mathrm{CoG}},y_{\mathrm{CoG}})$ are given by:
\begin{equation}
	x_{\mathrm{CoG}} =
	\frac{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i x_i}
	{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i},
	\qquad
	y_{\mathrm{CoG}} =
	\frac{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i y_i}
	{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i},
	\label{eq:cluster_VT}
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ and $y_i$ denote the position of pixel $i$, and $w_i$ is the pixel pulse height. Since the VT provides a digital signal, the pulse height is effectively binary, i.e. $w_i \in \left\{0,1\right\}$. Consequently, summing over the weights translates to finding the total number of pixels contributing to the cluster. The reconstructed position can therefore be interpreted as the average position of the pixels forming the cluster. On the other hand, the VT cluster uncertainties are estimated from the spatial spread of the contributing pixels, calculated using the variance of the mean formula:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_x^2 =
	\frac{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i
		(x_i-x_{\mathrm{CoG}})^2}
	{\displaystyle\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2},
	\qquad
	\sigma_y^2 =
	\frac{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i
		(y_i-y_{\mathrm{CoG}})^2}
	{\displaystyle\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}.
	\label{eq:cluster_VT_err}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is effectively the square of the number of pixels in the cluster. In my analysis, I also modified the VT uncertainty model by including off-diagonal terms of the covariance matrix, calculated as:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{xy} =
	\frac{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i
		(x_i-x_{\mathrm{CoG}})
		(y_i-y_{\mathrm{CoG}})}
	{\displaystyle\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}.
	\label{eq:cluster_VT_err_cov}
\end{equation}
The resulting covariance matrix of the reconstructed cluster position is therefore:
\begin{equation}
	\matr{\sigma}_{\mathrm{cluster}} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\sigma_x^2 & \sigma_{xy} \\
		\sigma_{xy} & \sigma_y^2
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{eq:VT_cov_mat}
\end{equation}
To account for the finite pixel size, a lower bound is imposed on the reconstructed cluster uncertainty using the digital readout limit:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\mathrm{digital}} =
	\frac{20.7~\mu\mathrm{m}}{\sqrt{12}},
\end{equation}
which is the uncertainty of a uniform distribution, where $20.7~\mu\mathrm{m}$ corresponds to the pixel pitch of MIMOSIS-28 sensors. The final cluster uncertainty is then defined by the final covariance matrix $\matr{\sigma}_{\mathrm{VT}}$:
\begin{equation}
	\matr{\sigma}_{\mathrm{VT}} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\max\left(\sigma_x^2,\sigma_{\mathrm{digital}}^2\right) & \sigma_{xy} \\
		\sigma_{xy} & \max\left(\sigma_y^2,\sigma_{\mathrm{digital}}^2\right)
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{eq:VT_final_err}
\end{equation}

Returning to the discussion of $\sigma_\text{pull}$, it is important to note that before the geometry and software-based alignment corrections, an even stronger dependence of the pull width on the fragment charge was observed than what shown in \autoref{fig:pull_width_charge} and \autoref{tab:vt_pull_sigmas}. For the lightest fragments, the pull standard deviation could reach values as large as $6$. The higher $\sigma_\text{pull}$ of lighter fragments are probably related to the broader angular distributions of low-charge fragments, which was shown in \autoref{fig:fluka_ekin_angle_dis} (left). As lighter fragments are emitted with larger scattering angles, their trajectories deviate more significantly from the beam direction, making the reconstruction more sensitive to imperfections in the detector geometry and uncertainty modeling. The alignment correction substantially reduced the charge dependence of the pull widths, improving the overall reconstruction performances. Nevertheless, the remaining excess of the pull widths for lighter fragments still indicates that the uncertainty model requires further refinement.

Several approaches can be considered to address the $\sigma_\text{pull}\gtrsim1$ issue and the associated VT error underestimation for lighter fragments. A straightforward solution would be introducing scale factors to the estimated uncertainties, which effectively scale the pull distributions toward the unit width. However, such scale factors should be regarded only as temporary corrections and necessitate a deeper understanding of their physics origin. A Monte Carlo study for the VT uncertainty model presented in \cite{Traini2026Meeting} showed that spatial resolution on the VT clusters degrades sharply as fewer pixels fire. This is exactly what happens for light fragments, since they deposit less charge. On one hand the larger clusters produced by heavier ions contain more geometrical information about the particle crossing point, allowing a more precise centroid estimate. Conversely, lighter fragments rarely activate peripheral pixels, generating narrower clusters that make the position estimate more sensitive to threshold fluctuations and charge diffusion processes. The current VT error model only partly accounts for the cluster size by including the pixel number in the denominator of \autoref{eq:cluster_VT_err}; however, it underestimates the position resolution yielding $\sigma_\text{pull}\gtrsim1$. Current investigation on the cluster size and shape (such as cluster elongation, thoroughly analyzed in \cite{Ma_czewski_2009} for MIMOSA-5 pixel sensors) are ongoing to solve the $\sigma_\text{pull}$ issue.
\subsubsection{Vertex Tracker plane rotation}\label{sssec:vt_plane_rotation}
To further investigate the $\sigma_\text{pull}\gtrsim1$ of lighter fragments, I conducted several studies concerning the VT plane rotation around the $z$ axis, which cannot yet be addressed by the software-based alignment procedure described in \autoref{sssec:software_based_alignment_procedure}. In this analysis, I expressed the VT $x$ and $y$ coordinate residuals (respectively $x_{\text{meas}}-x_{\text{fit}}$ and $y_{\text{meas}}-y_{\text{fit}}$) of each VT plane as a function of the VT cluster position $(x_{\mathrm{CoG}},y_{\mathrm{CoG}})$ defined in \autoref{eq:cluster_VT}, searching for possible rotations of the VT layers.

First, to motivate this study, let us consider how a plane rotation can be handled mathematically. Suppose that the VT sensor local axes $(x',y')$ are rotated by a small angle $\varphi$ with respect to the true experimental frame $(x,y)$. Then, a position measured from the rotated sensor frame of reference is quantified by:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{cases}
		x' = x\cos\varphi + y\sin\varphi, \\
		y' = -x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi,
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:VT_rotation}
\end{equation}
where the expressions stem from the algebra of rotation matrices. Assuming small rotation angles $\varphi\ll1$, \autoref{eq:VT_rotation} becomes:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{cases}
		x' \approx x+\varphi\cdot y, \\
		y' \approx y- \varphi\cdot x,
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:VT_rotation_small_angles}
\end{equation}
where the small angle approximation for the $\sin$ and $\cos$ functions are used. Although the physical hit/cluster position does not change, the axis along which the sensor measures it is tilted, yielding a residual of:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{cases}
		\text{residual}_x \equiv x' - x \approx \varphi \cdot y, \\
		\text{residual}_y \equiv y' - y \approx -\varphi\cdot x.
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:VT_rotation_residuals}
\end{equation}
Consequently, one should expect a dependence of $\text{residual}_x$ and $\text{residual}_y$ on the cluster position $(x_{\mathrm{CoG}},y_{\mathrm{CoG}})$, as it expressed in the true experimental frame $(x,y)$ (the global FOOT frame of reference). In particular, from \autoref{eq:VT_rotation_residuals}, the ``direct'' plots $\text{residual}_x$ vs $x_{\mathrm{CoG}}$ and $\text{residual}_y$ vs $y_{\mathrm{CoG}}$ should be flat; conversely, the ``cross'' plots $\text{residual}_x$ vs $y_{\mathrm{CoG}}$ and $\text{residual}_y$ vs $x_{\mathrm{CoG}}$ should display a clear trend with slopes of opposite sign and similar magnitude.

The plots contained in \autoref{fig:resx_clusposx_VT1}, \autoref{fig:resy_clusposy_VT1}, \autoref{fig:resx_clusposy_VT1} and \autoref{fig:resy_clusposx_VT1} precisely confirm the expectations. Conversely, \autoref{fig:resx_clusposy_VT1} and \autoref{fig:resy_clusposx_VT1} have opposite slopes with an approximate inclination of $\pm10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}$, corresponding to $0.05$--$0.06^\circ$.

\section{Optimization of the Microstrip Silicon Detector}\label{sec:optim_msd}
This section delineates the pull studies for the Microstrip Silicon Detector (MSD, see \autoref{sssec:microstrip_silicon_detector}), which pointed out several issues concerning the position uncertainty assigned to measurements by MSD planes. After outlining the position and error estimators originally employed in the MSD, novel estimators based on the MSD strips charge sharing are examined and compared with the previous ones.
\subsection{Microstrip Silicon Detector clustering algorithm}\label{ssec:msd_clustering_algorithm}
To estimate the particles' position, SHOE originally implements a clustering algorithm similar to the VT CoG computation: in both cases adjacent fired channels are grouped and the cluster position is obtained as a weighted average of the positions of its constituents. The key difference between the VT and MSD algorithms lies in the readout, as the VT integrates pixels providing a digital, binary readout, while the MSD strip technology implements an analog readout. Since each MSD readout strip can directly measure the energy deposited by the traversing particles, one can measure the charge sharing among neighboring strips to obtain a position estimate preciser than the binary reading of the VT.

As stated in \autoref{sssec:microstrip_silicon_detector}, each MSD sensor measures a single coordinate ($x$ or $y$), meaning that the cluster position is one-dimensional and can be computed in the local reference frame of each MSD plane as:
\begin{equation}
	x_{\mathrm{CoG}} =
	\frac{\displaystyle\sum_i E_i x_i}
	{\displaystyle\sum_i E_i},
	\label{eq:cluster_MSD}
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ is the central position of the $i$-th strip and $E_i$ is the energy loss measured in that strip (evaluated in ADC units), which plays the role of the weight $w_i$ of \autoref{eq:cluster_VT}. The sum in \autoref{eq:cluster_MSD} runs over a group of strips defined by a double-threshold clustering algorithm described in the caption of \autoref{fig:msd_clus_algo}, One of my first MSD updates was limiting the sum of \autoref{eq:cluster_MSD} only for strips with $E_i>0$.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{msd_clus_algo.pdf}
	\caption[MSD double-threshold clustering algorithm]{Double-threshold clustering algorithm used to build a MSD cluster. First, a strip exceeding the ``seed'' threshold is selected. Then, strips from the left/right overpassing the ``fire'' threshold are added in the cluster, skipping noisy or dead strips. Finally, the first right (left) strip below the fire threshold is added. At present, only strips with $E_i>0$ are integrated in the cluster. From \cite{Zarrella2024meeting}.}
	\label{fig:msd_clus_algo}
\end{figure}
The original MSD position uncertainty is estimated from the energy-weighted spread of the strips around the cluster position:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_x^2 =
	\frac{\displaystyle\sum_i E_i
		(x_i-x_{\mathrm{CoG}})^2}
	{\displaystyle\sum_i E_i}.
	\label{eq:cluster_MSD_err}
\end{equation}
As in the VT case, a lower bound is imposed to account for the finite strip size. This is especially relevant for clusters made of a single strip, for which the spread in \autoref{eq:cluster_MSD_err} vanishes. The bound is the uncertainty of a uniform distribution over the MSD readout pitch (digital readout limit):
\begin{equation}
	\sigma^{150}_{\mathrm{digital}} =
	\frac{150\,\mu\mathrm{m}}{\sqrt{12}},
	\label{eq:MSD_digital_readout_limit}
\end{equation}
where $150\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ is the pitch of the MSD strips. The final cluster uncertainty is therefore:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\mathrm{MSD}} =
	\max\left(\sigma_x,\sigma^{150}_{\mathrm{digital}}\right).
	\label{eq:MSD_final_err}
\end{equation}

Recall that the MSD structure (see \autoref{fig:msd2}) integrates $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$-distant floating strips that separate the neighboring readout strips, being $150\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ apart. Several studies \cite{ADRIANI199776,ALPAT2010207} report that the floating strip solution, combined with the MSD analog readout, can further reduce the position uncertainty. To this regard, \cite{SILVESTRE2023167717} shows that the MSD error can move towards the digital readout limit calculated with the floating strip pitch ($50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) rather than the readout strip pitch ($150\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) as in \autoref{eq:MSD_digital_readout_limit}:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma^{50}_{\mathrm{digital}} =
	\frac{50\,\mu\mathrm{m}}{\sqrt{12}}.
	\label{eq:MSD_digital_readout_limit_floating}
\end{equation}
This means that the current MSD uncertainty evaluation (given by \autoref{eq:MSD_final_err}) is not fully optimized, leaving room for improvement. This consideration motivated the study of a new clustering algorithm, which is be presented in the following sections.
