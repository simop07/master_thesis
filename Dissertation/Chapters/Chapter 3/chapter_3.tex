\chapter{Implementation of the trigger board firmware for the new Vertex Tracker}\label{ch:trigger_board}
As detailed in \autoref{ssec:tracking_reg}, among the tracking stations of the FOOT electronic setup, the Vertex Tracker (VT) fulfills the fundamental aim of reconstructing the interaction vertex with high spatial precision ($\simeq5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), which is provided by the MIMOSA-28 pixel technology. Nevertheless, the FOOT Collaboration is currently upgrading the VT with the cutting-edge MIMOSIS-2.1 technology, which offers a faster readout time without degrading the spatial resolution \cite{Rabaglia_2026}. This improvement turns out to be essential for increasing the data volume collected by the FOOT experiment, which is required to achieve the high-precision cross section measurements aimed by FOOT. The objective of this chapter is to present the implementations of the trigger board firmware for the new VT detector. This description is preceded by a brief discussion on the main features of the new MIMOSIS-2.1 technology.
\section{The new MIMOSIS-2.1 Vertex Tracker}\label{sec:new_vertex_tracker}
The FOOT Collaboration has planned to update the MIMOSA-28 (M28) VT detector (see \autoref{sssec:vertex_tracker}) with the more advanced MIMOSIS-2.1 chips, developed for the Micro Vertex Detector (MVD) of the CBM experiment at FAIR/GSI. While both rely on the CMOS Monolithic Active Pixel Sensors (MAPS) technology to minimize the material budget (see \autoref{fig:maps}), the MIMOSIS-2.1 sensor encompasses a matrix of $1024\times504$ pixels, each featuring an asymmetric pitch of $27\times30\,\mu\mathrm{m}^2$ ensuring a high spatial accuracy of $5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Similarly to the MIMOSA-28, the MIMOSIS-2.1 is a data push architecture, however the latter uses a global shutter technique rather than a rolling shutter (see \autoref{sssec:vertex_tracker}) method. Instead of employing a serial reading, the global shutter technique ensures that all pixels acquire the signal at the same time. The pixel signals are then sent to a local memory pending an external read request. This mechanisms cuts down reading time producing a fast temporal resolution of $5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

In addition to the enhanced spatial and temporal resolutions, the MIMOSIS-2.1 features high radiation tolerance, withstanding up to $5\,\mathrm{Mrad}$\footnote{$1\,\mathrm{rad}=0.01\,\mathrm{Gy}$, see \autoref{ssec:dose_definition}.} and $7\cdot10^{13}\,\mathrm{neq/cm}^2$ per operational layer \cite{Darwish_2023}. Even after irradiation, the MIMOSIS-2.1 sensor demonstrated $\simeq100\%$ detection efficiency and a spatial resolution between $4$--$6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ \cite{Darwish_2023}. Compared to the earlier prototypes, MIMOSIS-2.1 also integrates on-chip grouping circuits and presents an improved analog power grid \cite{deveaux2025beamperformancesmimosis21cmos}. \autoref{tab:mimosis_2.1} summarizes the main features of MIMOSIS-2.1, which make this technology a robust high-rate and low-material budget tracking sensor suitable for the high-radiation tolerance and high-precision environments typical of heavy-ion collision experiments.

Two technical justifications guided the selection of the MIMOSIS-2.1 technology for the new VT, namely the faster readout time, reduced from $185.6\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ of the original VT down to $5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and the radiation hardness. Both these improvements allow to increase the current data acquisition rate ($\simeq1\,\mathrm{kHZ}$) at least by a factor $2$.\footnote{Indeed, at present the maximum DAQ rate capability is $5\,\mathrm{kHz}$ \cite{9442772}.} Such upgrade will raise the statistics without varying the irradiation beam time. Moreover, it will reduce the piled-up fragmentation events in the VT, further improving the overall detector efficiency. To better understand the direct impact that the VT update will have on FOOT experimental activities, consider the formulae in \autoref{eq:cross_section_single_differential}, representing the single differential fragmentation cross sections with respect to the fragment's kinetic energy $E_\text{kin}$ and emission angle $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\frac{\text{d}\sigma}{\text{d}E_\text{kin}}(Z,E_\text{kin})
		&=
		\frac{\Delta\Omega}{4\pi}
		\frac{Y(Z,E_\text{kin})-\text{Bkg}}
		{N_\text{p}\,N_\text{t}\,\Delta E_\text{kin}(E_\text{kin})\,
			\varepsilon(Z,E_\text{kin})},
		\\[1em]
		\frac{\text{d}\sigma}{\text{d}\Omega}(Z,\theta)
		&=
		\frac{Y(Z,\theta)\text{Bkg}}
		{N_\text{p}\,N_\text{t}\,\Delta\Omega(\theta)\,
			\varepsilon(Z,\theta)},
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:cross_section_single_differential}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $Y$ is the number of yields, namely the number of tracks reconstructed with charge $Z$, emission angle $\theta$ and kinetic energy $E\text{kin}$. Necessarily, the uninteresting, background noise events (Bkg) are subtracted to the $Y$;
	\item $N_\text{p}$ is the number of primary beam particles computed with the fragmentation trigger (see \autoref{sssec:tdaq_foot});
	\item $N_\text{t}=\rho\,\Delta z\,N_A/A$ is the target's nuclear density, with the usual meaning of the symbols. Precisely, $\Delta z$ is the target's thickness (the $z$-axis follows the beam direction) and, for the data analyzed in this thesis, a graphite target (\ce{C}) is used, with $\Delta z=0.5\,\mathrm{cm}$, $\Delta x=\Delta y=0.5\,\mathrm{cm}$, $\rho=1.83\,\mathrm{g/cm}^3$ and $A=12.0107$;
	\item $\Delta E_\text{kin}$ and $\Delta\Omega$ are phase space factors respectively for the fragment's kinetic energy and emission angle;
	\item $\varepsilon$ is the convolution of the efficiencies affecting cross section measurements. $\varepsilon$ is influenced by the goodness of fragment identification SHOE reconstruction algorithm (see \autoref{sec:shoe}), the detector's angular acceptance, the physical efficiency of each FOOT sensor and the reliability of Monte Carlo simulations.
\end{itemize}
The increase of statistics induced by the MIMOSIS-2.1 VT will increment the number of correctly reconstructed tracks $Y$. As nuclear fragmentations occur in just $\simeq1\%$ of all the primary interactions, this contribution is of paramount importance. Furthermore, the additional experimental data will contribute to limit the statistical error on cross section measurements, laying the foundations for fulfilling the FOOT requirements on cross section uncertainties listed in \autoref{sec:foot_aim}. On the other hand, the pile-up reduction guaranteed by MIMOSIS-2.1 faster readout will improve the track discrimination, affecting directly the efficiency $\varepsilon$ and the Bkg terms.

To fully support the superior timing performances of the MIMOSIS-2.1, the FOOT Collaboration has implemented a new dedicated Trigger and Data AcQuisition (TDAQ) system for the incoming Vertex Tracker. After a brief description of the new VT TDAQ architecture, the following sections will focus on the VT trigger (see \autoref{sec:trigger_box}), being the module I mainly contributed to during my thesis.
\subsection{TDAQ architecture for the new Vertex Tracker}\label{ssec:TDAQ_newVT}
\autoref{fig:trg_box_mimosis_daq} illustrates the main components of the VT TDAQ architecture, composed of all the boards, wires and connectors managing the data flow and the trigger/busy conditions. To understand the TDAQ system operation, let us follow the chronological data flow, supported by the help of \autoref{fig:trg_box_mimosis_daq}. First, when a charged particle passes through a single MIMOSIS-2.1 sensor, the hit data are transferred via HDMI differential signaling to the $2$ custom-developed DAQ boards in \autoref{fig:trg_box_mimosis_daq} (top left). Successively, the DAQ boards convert the signals into the single-ended standard, before transmitting them to the ``DAQ'' DE10-Nano board displayed in \autoref{fig:trg_box_mimosis_daq} (top left), which houses a Intel/Altera System-on-Chip (SoC) FPGA (Cyclone V) with a dual-core Cortex-A9 CPU. This DE10-Nano fulfills several duties: it generates the clock signal for the PB, handles the PB-chip communication via the I${}^2$C protocol and, most importantly, deserializes the received data before temporarily storing it on a DDR3 SDRAM. Once stored, the data signals wait for an external trigger, which is provided by the custom-designed DE10-Nano trigger board (hereafter referred to as Trigger Box or TrgBox) in \autoref{fig:trg_box_mimosis_daq} (right). The TrgBox collects the trigger signals raised by the WaveDAQ system (see the FOOT triggers in \autoref{sssec:tdaq_foot}) and distributes it to the DAQ boards via LVDS standard. After accepting the trigger signal, the DAQ DE10-Nano board opens a readout window centered on the trigger time, encompassing all the data frames and the respective metadata that will be sent to the FOOT central DAQ.

\section{Trigger Box for the new Vertex Tracker}\label{sec:trigger_box}
\subsection{General overview}\label{ssec:TB_general_overview}

As described in \autoref{sssec:tdaq_general_info}, the synchronization of a TDAQ architecture is fundamental to associate the data fragments to the physics event that generated them. This aspect becomes critical especially in experiments like FOOT, where the number of detectors and their heterogeneity can impact the timing performances as well as the data quality. For this reason, the TrgBox aims to correctly forward a triggering signal from the V2495 central trigger module to each VT MIMOSIS-2.1 sensor, minimizing the latency. This trigger distribution activity must be also adapted to the data acquisition chain: a busy condition on the MIMOSIS-2.1 sensors or a data overload in the VT DAQ hindering the SDRAM reading must immediately inhibit the trigger propagation, activating a temporary busy state until all subsystems become available again. Both the trigger/busy signal distribution and the trigger validation logic are implemented in the TrgBox FPGA firmware, which is developed in VHSIC Hardware Description Language (VHDL).

The TrgBox requirements well align with the seamless integration between the FPGA and the HPS offered by the DE10-Nano board. On one hand, the inherent reprogrammability of FPGA systems provides the ultra-low latency, the direct I/O communication and the parallel processing typical of hardware resources, while retaining the reconfigurability of software development; such flexibility is essential in a dynamic experimental environment like FOOT. On the other hand, the minimal Linux operating system hosted by the CPU processor (HPS) can access the TrgBox FPGA firmware, employing \texttt{C++} software that reads and manipulates the FPGA registers. Indeed, the FPGA peripherals can be controlled and monitored by the CPU through memory-mapped registers over the so-called HPS-FPGA bridge.

The HPS is particularly important for the online and offline monitoring of the FPGA operation, as it allows to read from a computer screen the metadata attached to each event (including trigger tags like number, source and timestamp). This supplementary information is added via firmware to verify the data timing alignment and to look for possible temporary issues in the trigger distribution. In the following, a general description of the TrgBox FPGA firmware is provided, while more details on the HPS-FPGA interface can be found in \cite{thesisSavarese}.
\subsection{General firmware operation and personal contributions}\label{ssec:firmware_operation_signals_pers_contr}
The TrgBox firmware implements a Finite State Machine (FSM) characterized by three principal states, namely \texttt{IDLE} (which gives the initial synchronization), \texttt{CONFIG} (employed to configure the TrgBox registers) and \texttt{RUN} (used when signals are actively distributed among devices). During operation, the TrgBox handles $4$ main signals in input and output, better specified in \autoref{ssec:signals_trgbox}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \texttt{Trigger} signal;
	\item \texttt{Busy} signal;
	\item Time Stamp (TS) signals, decomposed into:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \texttt{TSClock} signal;
		\item \texttt{TSReset} signal.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
To perform the trigger distribution, the TrgBox sends out $N=11$ signals to external devices, whose connection are realized via $8$ LVDS and $3$ LVTTL lines. In addition, the TrgBox includes $16$ $32$-bit configuration registers, which control the FSM and the \texttt{Trigger} generation, and $19$ $32$-bit monitoring registers, providing the firmware status and the event information. These registers are detailed respectively in \autoref{ssec:config_registers} and \autoref{ssec:monitor_registers}.

The TrgBox is operated with an internal clock of frequency $50\,\mathrm{MHz}$, and is endowed with GPIO lines for \texttt{Trigger}, \texttt{Busy} and TS signals, button keys that can activate manual triggers for testing, LEDs indicators for system status and multiple switches that can further implement control functions.

During my thesis work, I refined the TrgBox VHDL firmware by inserting a configurable show-ahead FIFO for the event readout (further discussed in \autoref{ssec:fifo}), I improved the temporary \texttt{Busy} activation after a \texttt{Trigger} assertion and I enhanced the overall synchronization of the event-building logic, for instance accepting \texttt{Trigger} signals only when the TrgBox FIFO is not empty. In addition, I overhauled the \texttt{C++} class \texttt{FpgaInterfaceTB.cpp} and the macro \texttt{TriggerBoxScope.cpp} that allow to control and visualize the configuration and monitoring registers via the HPS-FPGA interface.

The correct functioning of the TrgBox firmware has been verified by the FOOT Collaboration in Frascati laboratories \cite{Rabaglia2026BTF}. I have further validated the TrgBox firmware in \cite{simone_pasquini_2026_19881390}, where I developed a VHDL project that implemented a FPGA-based detector emulator to generate muon-like trigger signals.
\subsection{Input and output signals}\label{ssec:signals_trgbox} 
\subsubsection{Trigger signal}\label{sssec:trigger_signal}
The TrgBox can either generate triggers internally or distribute external ones, depending on the chosen configuration. The \texttt{Trigger} is a TTL $3.3\,\mathrm{V}$ signal with a minimum duration of $100\,\mathrm{ns}$. It is distributed to the connected external devices only when the FSM is in the \texttt{RUN} state. When the system is not in \texttt{RUN}, the \texttt{Trigger} input (either external or internal) is indeed ignored. The selection of the active output channels, as well as the number of devices receiving the \texttt{Trigger}, is controlled through a dedicated configuration register.
\subsubsection{Busy signal}\label{sssec:busy_signal}
Each external device connected to the TrgBox can generate a \texttt{Busy} signal, expressed as a TTL $3.3\,\mathrm{V}$ logic level, to indicate that it is temporarily unable to accept new Triggers. During the \texttt{RUN} state, a \texttt{Global Busy} output is generated as the logical \texttt{OR} of all the \texttt{Busy} signals coming from the enabled devices, the latter being specified by a dedicated configuration mask. When the system is in any state other than \texttt{RUN}, the \texttt{Global Busy} output is forced to a logic high level. Note that the \texttt{Busy} signal is unique among the system signals because it flows in the opposite direction with respect to the other input signals, namely from the detectors to the TrgBox.
\subsubsection{Time Stamp signals}\label{sssec:timestamp_signals}
\texttt{TSClock} and \texttt{TSReset} are synchronization signals either generated internally by the TrgBox or received externally. These signals are always distributed, independently of the FSM state. In particular:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the \texttt{TSClock} is a $3.3\,\mathrm{V}$ square wave with a nominal period of $1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Although it can be configured from the TrgBox firmware, it currently follows the $1\,\mathrm{MHz}$ frequency provided by the timestamp clock of the V2495 central trigger board. The \texttt{TSClock} is connected to a TS register that counts its rising edges starting from a \texttt{TSReset} assertion; this allows to measure the time duration of each \texttt{RUN} cycle.
	\item The \texttt{TSReset} is a $3.3\,\mathrm{V}$ signal, normally held low and asserted only during the transition from \texttt{CONFIG} to \texttt{RUN}. It is used to reset all counters, in particular timing ones, and to provide the initial register synchronization.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{FIFO readout logic}\label{ssec:fifo}
The TrgBox handles \texttt{Trigger} events through a First In, First Out (FIFO) queue (see \autoref{fig:fifo}). As shown in the configuration register number $1$ in \autoref{tab:config_registers}, the FIFO has several operation modes, which can be directly configured by the user. To understand the FIFo operating principles, it is necessary to introduce the \texttt{TrgReadAcknowledge} signal, which is fundamental to readout the FIFO events. The \texttt{TrgReadAcknowledge} is a $1$-tick pulse asserted whenever a read request (\texttt{rdreq}) is issued to the FIFO, that is, whenever the system intends to advance to the next FIFO entry. As \texttt{TrgReadAcknowledge} is asserted, the FIFO read pointer advances to the next entry. Note that this signal does merely acknowledges the readout of the ``current'' FIFO entry, rather than driving the stored FIFO data towards the output bus. This behavior is a consequence of the show-ahead mode operation of the FIFO, which is also named read-ahead or First-Word Fall-Through (FWFT) mode. In the show-ahead mode, the FIFO automatically outputs the first valid data word as soon as the FIFO is not empty, without requiring the assertion of a read request \texttt{rdreq} signal. In this configuration, the \texttt{rdreq} input is interpreted as a read acknowledge rather than as a conventional read request command, thereby providing the data in the same cycle in which they are requested \cite{intel_fifo}. The difference between the standard and the FWFT read operations is illustrated in \autoref{fig:fifo_modes}.

\subsection{Configuration registers}\label{ssec:config_registers}
Configuration registers define the operational behavior of the TrgBox and directly control the main firmware components. In particular, configuration registers allow the user to monitor the FSM transitions, selecting among the operating mode of \texttt{IDLE}, \texttt{CONFIG} and \texttt{RUN}. Furthermore, they define which external devices are enabled to receive triggers through a bitmap mask; if the device are unavailable, their corresponding \texttt{Busy} bit is raised to a logic high. In addition, configuration registers enable the internal or external \texttt{Trigger} and TS signals generation. Configuration registers are primarily implemented by the following firmware modules, designed through the $14.1$ Quartus software \cite{altera_quartus_ii_14_1}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \texttt{Main\_FSM.vhd}, controlling the FSM states.
	\item \texttt{TriggerControl.vhd}, enabling the various \texttt{Trigger} sources and validating the final \texttt{Trigger} output, providing its final distribution.
	\item \texttt{BusyControl.vhd}, which applies device masking and creates the \texttt{Global Busy} signal.
	\item \texttt{TimeControl.vhd}, which selects the TS sources.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Monitoring registers}\label{ssec:monitor_registers}
Monitoring registers provide visibility into the internal firmware state during operation. They are continuously updated and can be read by the control software for diagnostic, monitoring and data-quality purposes. In particular, monitoring registers report the firmware version number, the FSM state, the total number of generated, received and lost triggers, the duration of the \texttt{Busy} status of the $N=11$ connected devices, the time duration of the \texttt{RUN} state, which external device was the last to release its \texttt{Busy} condition once the system is ready again and, most importantly, the FIFO-registered information of the validated \texttt{Trigger} event. The complete list of the monitoring registers is illustrated in \autoref{tab:monitor_registers}.
